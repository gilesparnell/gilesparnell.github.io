Data Fabric

Large organisations don't have a data platform — they have dozens, accumulated over decades. A data fabric is the architecture pattern that stops fighting that reality: leave the data where it lives, and build one metadata-driven layer that finds, governs, and serves all of it.

↑ contents01The problem: silo sprawl

Every acquisition, department, and era of technology leaves behind another data store — each with its own copies, its own access rules, and its own version of the truth.

Fig 1 — point-to-point sprawl: N consumers × M silos, each line a bespoke pipeline someone maintains

×Nobody can find data

Discovery is tribal knowledge. "Ask Dave, he built that extract in 2019."

×Copies drift

The same "customer" exists in five stores with three definitions. Which number goes to the board?

×No lineage

A regulator (or an exec) asks "where did this figure come from?" — and the honest answer takes three weeks.

×Governance per silo

Access, quality, and privacy rules are re-implemented — differently — in every system. Audit is a nightmare.

Why it matters now: GenAI made this worse and more urgent. Models are only as good as the data you can find, trust, and legally use — so the silo problem became the blocker for every AI initiative on the roadmap.

↑ contents02The idea: connect, don't centralise

Decades of "migrate everything into one platform" programmes failed — there's always a next silo. The fabric inverts the approach: leave data where it lives, and weave one intelligent layer across all of it, driven by metadata.

Fig 2 — the fabric: one governed layer over data left in place; consumers get one front door
The one-sentence version

A data fabric is to your data estate what a single air-traffic-control tower is to a sky full of planes — nothing has to land at one airport, but everything becomes visible, governed, and routable from one place.

↑ contents03Problem → fabric answer

Can't trust dataLineage + data contracts + quality monitoring — provenance is traceable; producers are accountable for what they publish.

↑ contents04The GCP implementation

Google's fabric story centres on Dataplex — the catalogue/governance plane — over BigQuery and BigLake.

ProductRole in the fabric

↑ contents05The AWS equivalent

AWS assembles the same fabric from more, smaller parts — Glue and Lake Formation carry the metadata + governance load, with DataZone as the newer business-facing catalogue.

Amazon S3The storage substrate — the lake almost everything else sits over.
AthenaServerless SQL with federated query — query S3 and external sources in place.
Redshift (+ Spectrum)Warehouse; Spectrum extends queries over S3 data without loading it.

QuickSightBI on top, with Q for natural-language querying.
Cross-cloud cheat: Dataplex ≈ Glue Catalogue + Lake Formation + DataZone. BigQuery ≈ Redshift/Athena. Dataflow ≈ Glue ETL/Kinesis Data Analytics. Pub/Sub ≈ Kinesis. Analytics Hub ≈ DataZone's data-product exchange. Same fabric, different lego.

↑ contents06Fabric vs mesh vs lakehouse — 20-second answers

≠Data mesh

Mesh is organisational — domain teams own their data as products. Fabric is technological — the metadata layer that makes any operating model workable. They're complementary: a mesh usually runs on a fabric.

≠Lakehouse

A storage architecture — warehouse features (ACID, governance) on lake economics via open table formats. A lakehouse is one (big) node the fabric connects; it doesn't solve discovery or estate-wide governance by itself.

↑ contents07Where the idea came from

"Data fabric" wasn't invented in one paper — it's the convergence of three older threads, renamed and upgraded by metadata.

EraWhat happened
1990s–2000sEnterprise Data Warehouse era — "copy everything into one governed store." Worked until data volume, variety, and SaaS sprawl outran any single store.
Mid-2000sData virtualisation (Composite Software, Denodo) — "query it where it lives" arrives, but without governance or discovery around it.
2010sData lake era — cheap storage for everything raw; many became ungoverned "data swamps." The pain that made governance the headline problem.
~2016Forrester analysts coin "big data fabric"; NetApp uses "data fabric" for hybrid-cloud storage (a different, infrastructure-level meaning — still causes confusion).
2019–2022Gartner era: data fabric named a top strategic technology trend (2022), redefined around active metadata — the catalogue stops being documentation and starts driving automation.
2023–nowGenAI forcing function — every vendor rebrands toward fabric (Microsoft literally names its analytics platform "Fabric"); AI-readiness becomes the business case that finally funds governance.

Name-drop wisely: "fabric" is also a marketing umbrella — the same word sells Microsoft's SaaS bundle, Informatica's integration suite, and NetApp's storage. Saying "fabric is a pattern, not a product — most real estates assemble it from a catalogue, a governance plane, and federation" instantly separates you from candidates who think it's something you buy in a box.

↑ contents09Pros, cons, and honest criticisms

✓ No big-bang migration

Value starts while legacy stays running — incremental onboarding, retire systems on merit, not on faith.

✓ Governance that scales

Policy defined once beats policy re-implemented per silo — the only workable answer at regulatory scale.

✓ AI-ready by construction

Discoverable, permissioned, lineage-backed data is precisely what GenAI programmes stall without.

✓ Buys optionality

Consumers bind to the fabric, not to sources — swapping a warehouse or absorbing an acquisition stops being a consumer-facing event.

× Metadata garbage-in

The fabric is only as good as its metadata. Auto-discovery gets you 60%; the rest is curation work organisations chronically underfund.

× Integration complexity

Assembling catalogue + governance + federation from multiple vendors is its own programme — the fabric can become the next silo of effort.

× Federation isn't free

Query-in-place performs worse than co-located data for heavy analytics — real fabrics still move hot data; judgement required.

× Doesn't fix ownership

The sharpest criticism: a fabric makes ownership visible, but only the operating model makes it real. Tech without stewardship = an expensive, accurate map of chaos.

The mesh-camp critique (worth acknowledging in interviews): data-mesh advocates argue fabric is too technology-centric — a central platform team's dream that under-invests in domain accountability. The mature answer: they're solving different layers of the same problem, and serious estates end up with both — fabric tech, mesh operating model.

↑ contents11The rollout programme — what a fabric build actually looks like

Not a platform project with a go-live — a multi-stream programme that earns adoption wave by wave. The cardinal rule: ship a thin vertical slice first, then scale by onboarding waves.

Fig 3 — five streams, three phases: governance and platform start together; everything converges on one end-to-end slice before scaling
Why slice-first beats platform-first: the #1 fabric failure mode is 12 months of platform build with zero consumers — governance debated in the abstract, value invisible, sponsors gone by month 9. One working slice forces every hard question early (ownership, contracts, access, performance) on a scope small enough to fix, and buys the political capital the waves need.

↑ contents12Programme rescue — reading a failing fabric

Fabric programmes rarely fail loudly — they fail quietly, in recognisable signatures. Diagnosis first; the fix follows from which signature you find.

Failure signatureWhat you'll observeRescue priority
Platform-first driftImpressive infra, no consumers; demos show tooling, never a business outcomeStop platform scope. Force one slice to a real consumer in 60–90 days — nothing else earns headcount until it lands.
Catalogue ghost townCatalogue deployed; nobody publishes, nobody searches; metadata stale within weeksOwnership, not features: named owners for the top 20 datasets, stewardship in role descriptions, publishing wired into the pipeline path so it can't be skipped.
Governance theatrePolicies documented, boards meet — but access still granted ad hoc and quality unmeasuredPick 3 policies that matter (PII access, quality SLAs, retention); enforce them in tooling on slice datasets; retire the paper.
Onboarding stallFirst sources took months each; wave plan assumes weeks; backlog quietly growingTreat onboarding as a product: repeatable kit (templates, contracts, runbook), measure cost-per-source, industrialise before scaling the promise.
Exec confidence goneSteering skipped by seniors, funding challenged, five competing status decksRe-baseline honestly: one truth deck, credible dates with change control, a visible quick win inside 6 weeks — confidence follows evidence, not promises.

↑ contents13The rescue playbook — questions first, then moves

The first two weeks are diagnosis. These questions find where truth lives — and each maps to a tactical move.

Probing questionWhat it exposesTactical move that follows
"Show me the last consumer who got value — walk me through their journey end-to-end."Whether anything real has shipped, and where the journey breaksIf no answer exists: declare the slice; scope it to 2–3 sources and one named consumer.
"Who owns this dataset — and what happens when its quality drops?"Whether ownership is real or nominalOwnership register for the top 20 datasets; unowned = flagged red at steering until claimed.
"How does a decision get made here — show me the last three big ones."Decision rights and traceability (usually the rot's centre)Decision log + decision-rights matrix; steering redesigned so risks arrive with costed options.
"What does it cost, in days, to onboard one source — and is it getting cheaper?"Whether the scaling promise is arithmetic or hopeOnboarding kit + cost-per-source metric on the WBR; no wave commitments until the number trends down.
"Which report do the execs actually trust?"Reporting fragmentation and where confidence leaksOne flash report, one truth deck; kill the competing versions in week one.
"What did the programme promise at funding — and what's been quietly dropped?"The gap between the business case and the plan of recordHonest re-baseline with change control; scope drops become recorded decisions, not silent drift.
"Where's the AI stream getting its data today?"Whether AI work is on-fabric or scraping around it (a quiet indictment)Route one AI use case through the fabric as a second slice — it's the sharpest proof the fabric works.
Sequencing logic (why this order): truth before plans (you can't re-baseline what you can't see) → ownership before tooling (features without owners decay) → one slice before waves (credibility funds scale) → rhythm throughout (WBR + decision log from day one, because the rescue itself must be visibly governed — the programme's governance is the product).

↑ contents14The delivery leader's lens

Why this pattern is as much a governance programme as an engineering one.

A fabric programme succeeds or fails on exactly the things delivery governance cares about: ownership (every dataset needs an accountable owner or the catalogue is a graveyard), contracts (producers keeping schema/quality promises — and a process for when they break), traceability (lineage is to data what a decision log is to a programme), and adoption (a fabric nobody publishes to is shelfware — the operating rhythm that gets teams onboarding datasets is the delivery job).

That's why these transformations run as multi-stream programmes — platform build, source onboarding waves, governance uplift, AI enablement — with heavy cross-stream dependencies. The architecture is the easy half; the governance maturity to operate it is where programmes wobble, and where delivery leadership earns its keep.

↑ contents15How it's actually wired — the technical planes

The fabric is a layer of indirection, but not a middleware box in the data path. It's a control plane of four cooperating parts; enforcement is pushed down into the engines, and queries never flow "through" the fabric itself.

Roles, not products

In Fig 2, the bottom row is where data rests (supply) and the top row is workloads that read it (demand). One product can play both roles — BigQuery is a store and the engine an analyst's dashboard queries. The fabric is the governed market between supply and demand: consumers ask for "customer revenue, governed", not "the table in warehouse X".

Fig 4 — control plane vs data path: the fabric is DNS + IAM for data, not a proxy carrying the packets
Why this matters in an architecture conversation: "doesn't the abstraction layer kill performance?" is the standard objection. The answer: the fabric isn't in the query path — policies are enforced by each engine, and access is either federated with predicate push-down (cold data) or materialised via pipelines (hot data), chosen per dataset on cost and SLO evidence. That federate-vs-materialise call is a running engineering judgement, not a one-off.
