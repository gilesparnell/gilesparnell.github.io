Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 07

Two lanes, one account

There are two different reasons to sell an option, and they need opposite rules. On the screen they look almost the same. That is exactly why people come unstuck.

What’s in here

  1. One question sorts every trade
  2. Lane A — companies you actually want
  3. Lane B — companies you just want money from
  4. What Lane B costs you
  5. Running both at once
01

One question sorts every trade

The one rule

If I’m assigned these shares, am I pleased?

Yes → Lane A. Sell a plain put, keep the cash ready, welcome the shares. No → Lane B. Never sell a plain put here — use a spread, so the loss has a floor.

Ask it before you look at what the option pays. The payment will always argue for “yes”, because a big payment is exactly what a risky company looks like.

Everything else on this page follows from your answer.

02

Lane A — companies you actually want

Tesla, SpaceX, Nvidia — whatever your list is. Here you are not really chasing income. You are buying companies at prices you chose, and getting paid while you wait to find out whether you get them.

How it runs

1. Decide the price you’d genuinely be happy to pay for 100 shares. Not a strike off the screen — a price.

2. Sell a put at that level. You’re paid straight away. Put the full purchase money aside in your own head, whatever the broker holds.

3. If the price stays up, nothing happens. Keep the money, do it again.

4. If it falls, the shares turn up. That is not a mistake. That is the thing you asked for.

5. Now you own 100 shares, so you can sell a call against them and get paid a third time.

The reason this works is that the bad outcome isn’t really bad. You end up owning a company you wanted, at a price you picked. The loss is a purchase.

The version that goes wrong

You’re assigned at $95 and it keeps sliding to $70. You now hold shares worth $2,500 less than you paid. Nothing broke — the trade did exactly what it promised. The question is whether you still believe in the company. A lower price on its own is not new information. A changed business is. Don’t confuse the two, and don’t sell another put to “fix the average” — that just makes a bad position bigger.

03

Lane B — companies you just want money from

Different job entirely. You have no interest in owning these. You only want the payment. So the shares turning up is the thing you are trying to avoid.

A plain put can’t do that, because a plain put is a promise to buy. So you build it differently:

The two-part version

Sell a put, as normal. That’s the bit that pays you.

At the same time, buy a cheaper put lower down. That’s a safety net.

If the share price collapses, the one you bought goes up in value and stops the damage. Your loss has a floor, and you know exactly where it is before you start.

It’s called a spread — because you have two contracts spread apart on price.

  • Share at $100. Sell the $90 put, get paid $200.
  • Buy the $85 put for $80. Now you keep $120 rather than $200.
  • Gap between them: $5, which over 100 shares is $500.
  • Worst case: $500 minus the $120 you kept = $380. It cannot get worse than that.
  • Cash tied up: $380 — not the $9,000 a plain put would need.

That last line is the real reason to use one. The same money stretches across many more positions, which is what lets you spread across different companies at all on a normal-sized account.

04

What Lane B costs you

A floor under the loss sounds like it has no downside. It has several, and they’re worth knowing before you plan around it.

  • You lose the full amount fairly often. Capped is not the same as unlikely. Losing the whole $380 is a normal outcome, not a freak one.
  • You get paid less. $120 rather than $200, because you bought the safety net out of your own premium.
  • You may not be allowed to yet. Brokers put spreads at a higher permission level than plain puts. Worth checking yours.
  • Two contracts, double the costs. Two lots of commission, and two gaps between buy and sell price to cross, both going in and coming out.
  • Nothing to fall back on. In Lane A a bad day leaves you holding a company you wanted. Here it just leaves you poorer.
05

Running both at once

They compete for the same money, and the one with the better-looking payment will always win that argument in the moment. So decide the split when the market is shut.

  • Lane A: few companies, big commitments. The limit is how many assignments you could genuinely absorb at the same time.
  • Lane B: more companies, small commitments. The limit is attention — and the fact that twelve technology companies all fall on the same day.
  • Untouchable slice: money neither lane may use. The day everything drops together is the day Lane A hands you shares, Lane B hands you losses, and the best prices in a year appear at once.
The two ways this goes wrong

Lane B run like Lane A. Selling a plain put on a company you don’t want, because the payment looked good and it’s the habit you’re in. It falls, you own it, at full price, with no floor and no reason to keep it. This is the expensive one.

Lane A picked by payment. Letting the screen choose your companies. The richest payments belong to whatever the market is most frightened of — which is rarely the same list as the companies you researched and want to hold for years.
