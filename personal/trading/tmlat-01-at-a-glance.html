Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 01

What on earth is an option?

An option is a ticket. That is genuinely most of it. Everything else on this page is just detail about what the ticket says and who has to do what.

What’s in here

01

It’s a ticket, not a thing you own

When you buy a share, you own a slice of a company. When you buy an option, you don’t own anything of the sort. You own a ticket that says you’re allowed to do something later, at a price that’s agreed today.

Picture it like this

Your neighbour has a bike you like. It’s worth about £100 today. You give them £5 and they write you a note: “Giles is allowed to buy my bike for £100 any time in the next month.”

That note is an option. You paid £5 for it. You don’t own the bike — you own the right to buy the bike at £100.

If the bike suddenly becomes worth £150, your note is brilliant: you can buy at £100 and it’s worth £150. If the bike drops to £70, your note is useless — but you only ever lose the £5.

That’s the whole shape of it. A small payment now, in exchange for a choice later.

The words grown-ups use
Option
The ticket itself.
Premium
The £5 you paid for the ticket.
Underlying
The bike. The actual thing the ticket is about.
Strike
The £100 written on the ticket — the price you’re allowed to deal at.
Expiry
The date the ticket stops working.
02

Two kinds of ticket: calls and puts

There are exactly two kinds. That’s it — not twenty.

  • A call lets you buy at the set price. You want one if you think the price is going up, because buying cheap is good.
  • A put lets you sell at the set price. You want one if you think the price is going down, because selling high is good.
A way to remember it

Call — you can call the thing over to you. It comes to you. You’re buying.

Put — you can put the thing onto someone else. It goes away from you. You’re selling.

03

Somebody wrote that ticket — and they’re stuck with it

Here’s the bit people miss, and it’s the most important idea on this page. Every ticket has two people attached to it, and their situations are completely different.

  • The person who bought the ticket has a choice. They can use it or bin it. Worst case, they wasted what they paid.
  • The person who sold the ticket has a duty. If the other person decides to use it, they must do what it says. They don’t get a vote.
Back to the bike

Your neighbour took your £5. That was nice for them — free money, probably.

But if the bike shoots up to £300, they still have to sell it to you for £100. They’re not allowed to say “actually, no.” They took the money; they took the duty with it.

Why this matters more than anything else

Buying tickets means you can lose what you paid. Selling tickets means you can lose much more than you were paid. The £5 your neighbour pocketed doesn’t come close to covering a £200 problem. Almost every options disaster is really this idea, misunderstood.

The words grown-ups use
Holder / long
The person who bought the ticket and has the choice.
Writer / short
The person who sold the ticket and has the duty.
Exercise
Actually using your ticket.
Assignment
Being told you must keep your promise, because someone used the ticket you sold.
04

Four things you can do — that’s the lot

Two kinds of ticket, two sides to be on. Two times two is four. There is no fifth thing.

  • Buy a call — pay a little, hoping it goes up.
  • Sell a call — get paid a little, promising to hand something over if it goes up.
  • Buy a put — pay a little, hoping it goes down (or as insurance on something you own).
  • Sell a put — get paid a little, promising to buy something if it goes down.

When people describe complicated-sounding strategies with names like collar, spread or condor, they are always just doing several of these four at the same time. Nothing new gets invented.

05

Is my ticket worth anything right now?

At any moment you can ask a simple question: if I used this ticket this very second, would I be better off? That’s all “moneyness” means, despite sounding like a made-up word.

  • In the money — yes, using it right now would help. Your call lets you buy at £100 and the thing costs £130.
  • At the money — the ticket price and the real price are basically the same. Using it changes nothing.
  • Out of the money — no, using it would be silly. Your call lets you buy at £100 but the thing only costs £70. You’d just buy it normally.

Out of the money doesn’t mean worthless, though. There’s still time on the clock, and things move. That leftover value has its own explanation, which is No. 03.

06

One contract means one hundred

A small thing that trips everyone up early on: options are sold in packs. One contract is 100 shares, essentially always.

So when a screen says a contract costs £2.10, it does not cost £2.10. It costs £2.10 × 100 = £210. And when you promise to buy at a strike of £139, you’re promising £139 × 100 = £13,900.

Check this every single time

The prices you see quoted are per share, but you always trade in hundreds. Multiply by 100 before you decide anything. A trade that looks like pocket change is usually a four-figure commitment.
