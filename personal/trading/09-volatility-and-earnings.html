No. 06 — owning & earning

Earnings week is when option prices are fattest. That is the opportunity and the risk at the same time.

No. 06 lists earnings as a blackout — an event you will not write through. That is a sound default. This page is the informed exception: how to decide, deliberately and with numbers, whether to sell a covered call or a put across an earnings report, and what it will cost you if you do.

Why earnings week pays sellers

You are paid for the uncertainty, not the outcome.

Before the report nobody knows the result, so every option carries a large uncertainty charge. The seller collects that charge up front. The morning after, it is gone — whichever way the stock moved.

01 / Before you sell

Three numbers before you sell.

Every option price carries a forecast. Three numbers tell you whether that forecast is cheap or expensive right now.

NumberWhat it tells youHow a seller reads it
Implied volatility (IV)The swing the market is pricing in, as a yearly percentage.Higher means fatter premiums. Divide by 16 for a rough one-day move: IV 85% ≈ 5.3% a day.
IV / HVIV against how much the stock has actually been moving (historical volatility).Above 100%: options are priced for more than the stock has been doing. Earnings weeks often run 200%+.
IV RankToday’s IV against its own range over the past 52 or 13 weeks, from 0 to 100.70+ is a common rule of thumb for “expensive for this stock” — a seller’s window. A low rank means thin premiums.
Worked read

XYZ, the day before earnings

IV 85%. The stock’s recent actual swing (HV) is 35%. IV/HV ≈ 240%. IV Rank 80. All three say the same thing: option buyers are paying a lot for this week.

Where to find them — IBKR

Portfolio view → gear → Columns

Add Implied Vol. % (the IV of each position), Implied Vol./Hist. Vol % (the ratio) and 52 Week / 13 Week IV Rank. The option chain shows each expiry’s IV at the top of the strike column. Opt. IV% on the stock panel is one 30-day figure for the whole stock.

02 / The expected move

Read the expected move straight off the chain.

Add the at-the-money call to the at-the-money put for the expiry you are looking at. The total is roughly how far the market expects the stock to move, in either direction, by that date.

Inside the range

Firmly in play

The market already expects the stock could reach these strikes. Expect them to be tested.

Outside the range

Need a surprise

These are only reached if the report beats what everyone is already braced for. Choosing them is a bet on that surprise.

03 / The morning after

Why the premium is fat now, and gone tomorrow.

Before the report, nobody knows the result, so option prices carry a large uncertainty charge. The moment the result is out, that charge disappears. IV collapses overnight and every option on the stock gets cheaper at once. This is IV crush.

Timing

Sell before, not after

The uncertainty charge is only there while the uncertainty is. Waiting for the result means selling after the premium has gone.

The seller’s tailwind

The crush works for you

If the stock does not run past your strike, the option you sold loses most of its value overnight. Illustrative: a 210 call sold for $3.00 might be worth around $0.70 the next morning with the stock flat. Buy it back for $70, keep $230, and you are free to sell again.

Choosing the expiry

Shorter concentrates it

An expiry two days after the report might show IV of 85% while one three trading days later shows 66%. The event is a bigger share of the shorter option’s life. The longer one still pays a little more in dollars, for the extra days.

04 / Choosing the strike

Compare strikes by outcome, not by premium.

The closer the strike, the more premium you collect. But the strike is also your ceiling. The question is never “which pays most”, it is “which pays most for the outcome I actually expect”. XYZ at $200, shares owned at $200, Friday expiry two days after earnings:

StrikePremiumDelta ≈ chance called awayBest possible resultLosing money below
205$4000.40$900$196.00
210$3000.30$1,300$197.00
215$2200.23$1,720$197.80
220$1500.17$2,150$198.50

Best possible result = premium plus the share gain up to the strike. Losing money below = share cost minus premium per share.

Find the crossover.

For any two strikes there is a closing price where they pay exactly the same. Below it the closer strike wins; above it the further strike wins. 205 vs 210 cross at $206. 210 vs 215 at $210.80. 215 vs 220 at $215.70. Set those against the expected range of $187–$213: the 215 and 220 only come out ahead if the stock finishes near or beyond the top of what the market is pricing. That is a bet on a surprise — fine if you have a reason, expensive if you do not.

05 / No view?

If you don’t have a view, don’t pretend to.

Delta works the same for calls as for puts: it is roughly the chance the option finishes in the money. For a covered call, that is the chance your shares get called away. Higher delta, more likely. Lower delta, less likely. The reversed version circulates; check it against any live chain — the strike nearest the price always has the highest delta.

Your honest viewWhere to sellWhy
No real viewAround 0.30The neutral default. Most of the premium of a close strike, and a real chunk of upside kept.
Evidence for a rise — results, guidance, margins, not headlines0.20 – 0.25Leave room for the move you have a reason to expect.
Mainly want income, happy to be called away0.35 – 0.45Paid more precisely because it is more likely to be held to.
Headlines are priced.

A product launch everyone has seen, or a wave of media attention, is not an edge. Option prices already reflect what the whole market knows. If the reason for leaning bullish is the news cycle, sell the neutral strike.

06 / What it costs

What selling into earnings costs you.

  • A capped upside on a gap up. If the report sends the stock 10% higher overnight, you keep the gain only up to your strike. The rest belongs to the buyer.
  • A small cushion on a gap down. The premium covers only the first few dollars of a fall. Below your break-even you lose $100 for every $1, exactly as if you had never sold the call. You keep the shares and the premium, and nothing forces you to sell — but the next call pays less if you keep its strike at or above your cost.
  • One contract per 100 shares. Never more. A second call on 100 shares is uncovered (naked). Its loss has no ceiling, and earnings night is exactly when a stock gaps furthest. Most brokers restrict it to their highest permission level for that reason.

07 / The ticket

Before you press Submit.

  • The side panel shows the contract you meanSome platforms keep the last contract you clicked loaded in the order panel. Click the bid of the row you want, then read the panel header: expiry, strike, CALL or PUT.
  • The action is SELLA profile showing “max loss = premium, unlimited return” is the buyer’s view of that option. Use the Sell button and check the ticket says Sell.
  • Quantity = shares ÷ 100100 shares, 1 contract. Anything more is the naked call above.
  • Limit at the mid, not the askStart halfway between bid and ask. If it has not filled in a minute or two, step $0.05 toward the bid.
  • DAY orders cancel at the closeAn unfilled order disappears. You can place it again next session, still before the report.
  • Know the report time in your own timezoneA US after-the-close report lands the next morning in Australia.

The short version.

Earnings week pays sellers more than any other week of the quarter, because it is the week the outcome is least known. Read the three numbers, read the expected move, compare strikes by outcome, and sell the neutral strike unless you have real evidence. Nothing here is a recommendation to trade, and none of it is financial advice; it is a map of how the mechanics fit together if you choose to use them.
