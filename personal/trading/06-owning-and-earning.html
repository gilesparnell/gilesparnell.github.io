No. 02 — trading safely

The first five numbers treated options as trades. This one treats them as a way of managing shares you intend to own for years. The question changes from “which way will it move?” to something far more answerable: given what I already hold, which positions am I even allowed to take, and what does each one commit me to?

That reframing matters because it removes prediction from the decision. You are not forecasting a price. You are naming a price at which you would be content to buy, or content to sell, and being paid for the commitment.

The one rule

Only sell puts on things you’d want to own.

Selling a put is a promise to buy the shares. If the price falls you own them — that is the deal, not bad luck. This single rule is what turns an assignment from a loss into a purchase you were happy to make, and it is the condition every page here rests on.

01 / First fork

Cash backs one side. Shares back the other.

Before strategy, before strike selection, one structural fact decides everything downstream: an option you sell must be backed by the thing you have promised to deliver. That single rule splits the whole field in two.

You hold cash

Cash backs the buy promise

Selling a put promises to buy shares at the strike. What you must be able to deliver is money — 100 × strike per contract. Cash is the raw material of the put side, and its purpose is acquiring shares at a price you chose rather than the price on offer today.

Use it when: you want to own the stock, don’t yet (or want more), and the settlement money is genuinely available.

You hold shares

Shares back the sell promise

Selling a call promises to deliver shares at the strike. What you must be able to deliver is stock — 100 shares per contract, in the same account. Shares are the raw material of the call side, and their purpose is producing income from a position you already hold.

Use it when: you own at least 100 shares and would accept selling some of them at a higher price.

Everything follows from which of those two you have. Cash and no shares gives you exactly one income position — the short put. Shares and no spare cash gives you exactly one — the covered call. Hold both and the full set opens, including the cycle on page three that converts each into the other.

02 / The decision

Four tools. Your holdings decide which are available.

Every income strategy around a stock you like reduces to one of these. The left column is the honest question to ask first — if the answer isn’t a clear yes, the position isn’t for you, whatever the premium looks like.

If this is true of youThe positionBacked byWhat you are actually promising
I want more of this, but only lower — and I hold the cash to buy it. Sell a put Cash To buy 100 shares at the strike if it falls below. You keep the premium either way.
I own 100+ shares, want income, and would accept selling higher. Sell a covered call Shares To sell 100 shares at the strike if it rises above. Your upside stops there.

Read it twice.

The two rows that earn premium both hand someone else a decision about your money. The two that cost premium buy back that control. There is no row that earns income and keeps full control — that combination is what the premium is paid for.

03 / Shape of the payoff

What each income position looks like at expiry.

Both charts share the same silhouette, and it is worth sitting with: a flat ceiling, and a slope that keeps going. That asymmetry is the entire character of selling premium.

Short put · backed by cash

Break-even sits at strike minus premium — your effective purchase price if assigned. Everything above the strike pays the same flat amount, however far it rises.

Covered call · backed by shares

The premium lowers your break-even a little, but it does not protect you in a fall — you still own the shares all the way down. The shaded block is the rally you agreed to miss.

The symmetry.

Both cap your gain at the premium and leave a much larger adverse outcome open. That is not a flaw in the trade — it is what you are being paid for. Selling options transfers risk from someone else to you, and the premium is the fee for accepting it. Any strategy promising high recurring income with no matching exposure has simply hidden the exposure somewhere you haven’t looked yet.

05 / Collateral

The rules that decide what you can actually do.

Strategy discussions jump straight to strike selection. These four mechanical constraints decide whether a position is available to you at all, and they cause more unpleasant surprises than any amount of mispricing.

Constraint 01

One contract, one hundred shares

A covered call needs 100 shares per contract in the account. Holding 187 shares covers one contract, not one and a half. The remainder simply stays uncapped — which is often exactly what you want.

Constraint 02

Shares must sit at the same broker

Shares held at one broker cannot collateralise an option sold at another. A call written against shares held elsewhere is naked, not covered — a different risk profile entirely and usually a different approval level. Consolidate first, or don’t write the call.

Constraint 03

“Cash-secured” is a discipline, not a setting

In a margin account the broker typically reserves only a fraction of the assignment value — often around 20–30%, not the full amount. The platform will happily let you sell far more puts than your cash could ever settle. The cash backing is a rule you impose on yourself; nothing enforces it for you.

Constraint 04

Assignment can arrive early

US equity options are American-style: a short option can be assigned on any trading day, not only at expiry. It grows more likely as the option moves deep in-the-money or ahead of a dividend. Plan for the obligation landing sooner than the expiry date implies.

06 / The cycle

The wheel — and why you might want to slow it down.

Chain the two income positions together and you get a repeating cycle, commonly called the wheel. Cash becomes shares through assignment; shares become cash through a call; and round it goes, collecting premium at each turn.

The modification.

The wheel as usually taught assumes you are indifferent to holding the stock — it treats shares as inventory. If your actual thesis is that you want to own the company for years, a fast wheel works against you: aggressive calls keep selling the position back out at precisely the moment it is rising. Writing calls further out-of-the-money collects less premium and turns the wheel more slowly, which is the correct trade when the shares themselves are the point.

07 / Correlation

The trap: doubling down on what you already own.

Selling a put on a company you already hold heavily feels efficient — it is a stock you know, and you would be glad to own more cheaply. The problem is when the obligation triggers.

Assignment happens on a fall. That is precisely the moment your existing holding is worth less. So the position quietly commits you to adding capital to a losing concentration at the worst possible time, and it does so automatically, without any decision from you on the day. Two positions that look independent on paper turn out to be one bet, sized larger. Varying which underlying you write against is not a refinement — it is the point.

08 / Buying on purpose

When assignment is the point, not the risk.

Everything up to here treats assignment as something to be managed. For a company you have genuinely decided you want to own, that is backwards. Assignment is the objective — and once it is, the premium stops being income and turns into something else.

The reframe.

The premium is not revenue. It is a discount on the purchase price. Sell a $350 put for $456 and take the shares: you pay $35,000, you kept $456, so the real cost is $34,544 — $345.44 a share. The premium came off the price. It was never a separate income stream sitting alongside the purchase; it was always part of what you paid.

That resolves the tension on the page before. “Collect the most premium” and “actually end up owning it” look like competing goals, and for a company you don’t want, they are. For a company you do want they collapse into one: the lowest net price for shares you finish up holding. Which means writing closer to the current price rather than further from it — paying more premium and making assignment more likely at the same time. For once, both goals point the same way.

09 / The dial

Delta tells you which business you are actually in.

Delta is roughly the chance the option finishes in the money, which is roughly the chance you end up owning the shares. It is the single number that says whether a position is an income trade or an acquisition trade — whatever you intended when you opened it.

DeltaChance of getting the sharesPremiumWhat that position actually is
0.10 – 0.15Around 1 in 8SmallIncome only — you will rarely own anything
0.20 – 0.30Around 1 in 4ModeratePatient accumulation, over many months
0.30 – 0.45Around 1 in 3GoodGenuinely trying to acquire the shares
About 0.50Roughly a coin flipHighAt the money — aggressive acquisition
Above 0.50More likely than notHighestStrike above the price — deliberate buying
Read it honestly.

If you say you want the shares and every open position sits at 0.10, the positions disagree with you. There is nothing wrong with a 0.10 delta put — it is simply an income trade, and it will almost always leave you holding cash rather than stock. The mismatch is worth catching early, because a year of it means a year of small premiums and no shares.

10 / Following through

Four things that decide whether you actually end up owning it.

The aggressive route

Write above the price

A strike above today’s price is already in the money, so assignment is likely and the premium is large. Shares at $150, sell the $155 put for $8: if assigned you pay $155 having kept $8, so $147 net — below what the shares cost today. Paid to commit, and bought at a discount to the screen.

The discipline

Don’t roll away from it

When it goes in the money the platform shows a loss and every instinct says roll it further out. For a company you wanted, take the shares. That was the point. Rolling to dodge assignment is how people finish with neither the stock nor the premium — and if you find yourself rolling, the honest question is whether you wanted the company at all.

The trap afterwards

Go carefully on covered calls

The obvious next move is to sell calls against the new shares. That is the wheel, and it is built to churn stock — wrong tool if the plan was to keep it. One good run and the shares are called away at $400 on the way to $600, leaving you in cash in the exact company you spent months acquiring. If you write calls here at all, use a strike you would be genuinely pleased to sell at.

The real limit

Count the assignments you can fund

Raising delta only works if you can pay when it lands. Correlated names fall together, so assume every position triggers in the same week and ask whether the cash covers it. Size to the number of assignments you can actually absorb, accumulate in tranches at different strikes and dates, and keep a slice of cash that no strategy may touch.

The honest cost.

If you truly want to own something, the certain way to own it is to buy it. Selling puts gets it slightly cheaper if it comes to you, and leaves you holding cash if it doesn’t. In a year where the share runs from $375 to $600, the put seller collects a few hundred dollars a month and never gets aboard. That is the actual cost of this approach, and it appears on no screen anywhere — there is no line item for the shares you didn’t buy.

11 / The objective

Two goals that quietly fight each other.

“Generate as much monthly income as possible without risking the capital” sounds like one objective. It is two, pulling in opposite directions. Every lever that raises premium raises risk somewhere:

Lever

Closer strikes

More premium, and a much higher chance of assignment. You are paid more precisely because you are more likely to be held to it.

Lever

More contracts

Income scales linearly; so does the obligation. Committing all available cash leaves nothing for the opportunity a fall creates.

Lever

Higher volatility names

Rich premium is compensation for real movement. The market is not mispricing it for your benefit.

A more honest objective, and a more achievable one: generate repeatable income while preserving the long-term thesis. That version has a defensible answer to “should I take this trade?” — does it threaten the holding I actually care about? The maximising version answers every such question with “yes, take it”, which is how income strategies end in forced sales.

12 / The system

Write the rules before the chain is in front of you.

Strike selection under live quotes is where discipline dies — the premium is visible and the risk is abstract. Deciding the rules in advance, away from the screen, is the whole game. Fill this in once, then trade it.

13 / Before you write

The pre-flight checks.

  • Am I looking at live prices?Delayed quotes on an option with a wide spread means pricing blind. Confirm the data is real-time before setting any limit.
  • Would I be pleased to be assigned at this effective price?If the honest answer is no, the premium is irrelevant. This is the only question that really matters.
  • Is the collateral genuinely there, in the right account?Cash for puts; 100 shares per contract, same broker, for calls.
  • Does this concentrate something I already hold?Check what else moves with it. Correlated positions are one bet, not two.
  • Is the contract liquid?Tight spread, real volume, healthy open interest — otherwise exiting early costs more than the premium was worth.
  • Do I know the tax treatment?Premium received and assignment both carry consequences, differing by jurisdiction and by whether you are classed as investor or trader. Settle it with an accountant before volume builds, not at year end.
The short version.

Only sell puts on things you’d want to own. Beyond that: let your holdings decide which positions are available — cash writes puts, shares write calls. Name the price you would be content to transact at, back the obligation with real collateral, and write the rules before you open the chain. Income follows from the discipline, not from the strike. Nothing here is a recommendation to trade, and none of it is financial advice; it is a map of how the mechanics fit together if you choose to use them.
