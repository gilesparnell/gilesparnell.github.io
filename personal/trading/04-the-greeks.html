No. 03 — pricing

The Greeks sound like advanced maths. They're not. Each one just answers a plain question: "if this one thing moves a little, how much does my option's price move?" You never calculate them — your platform prints them next to every option. Learn to read them as rates and dials, and a position stops being a mystery.

01 /
What
they
are

Five dials on one option.

An option's price is pushed around by a handful of forces — the stock, the clock, and the market's nerves. Each Greek measures sensitivity to exactly one of them, holding the others still.

GreekAnswers the question…Reacts to
Delta  Δ"If the stock moves $1, how much does my option move?"Stock price
Gamma  Γ"…and how fast is that delta itself changing?"Delta
Theta  Θ"If one day passes and nothing else changes, what do I lose?"Time
Vega  V"If implied volatility rises 1%, how much does my option move?"Volatility
Rho  ρ"If interest rates shift, how much does my option move?"Rates
The mindset.

Don't memorise formulas. Learn the direction and size of each dial for the position you hold — "decay hurts me, a bigger stock move helps me, rising volatility helps me a little." That sentence is you reading your Greeks. The next pages give you the feel of each.

02 /
Delta

Delta — how much you move with the stock.

The most useful Greek, and the one to learn first. A call's delta runs from 0 to +1; a put's from 0 to −1. It has three readings at once — and all three are handy.

A call's delta across moneyness
Reading 1 · Rate

A delta of 0.50 means the option gains about $0.50 for every $1 the stock rises (×100 = $50 per contract).

Reading 2 · Shares

It's your share-equivalent exposure. A 0.50-delta call behaves like owning ~50 shares right now.

Reading 3 · Odds

Roughly the option's chance of finishing in the money. A 0.30 delta ≈ a 30% shot, loosely. A handy gut-check.

Why it matters for safety: delta tells you your real exposure. A cheap-looking deep-in-the-money call with 0.90 delta moves almost dollar-for-dollar with 90 shares — far more risk than the small premium suggests. Always know the delta, not just the price.

03 /
Gamma

Gamma — how fast delta changes.

If delta is your speed, gamma is your acceleration. It tells you how much delta itself will shift as the stock moves. Gamma is highest for at-the-money options close to expiry — which is exactly why last-week, at-the-money positions feel so wild.

High gamma

Near expiry, at the money. Delta can swing from 0.3 to 0.7 on a small move — your exposure lurches around and the position whipsaws. Thrilling, and hard to manage.

Low gamma

Far from expiry, or deep in/out of the money. Delta drifts slowly, so the position behaves predictably. Calmer — usually better while learning.

Takeaway: the "cheap" same-day and same-week options that tempt beginners are high-gamma powder kegs. Steadier, longer-dated positions trade a little cost for a lot less lurch.

04 /
Theta

Theta — the daily rent.

You met this force in No. 03 as time decay; as a Greek it's just the dollar amount of time value that evaporates per day. The sign is what matters: it flips depending on which side you're on.

Buyers · theta negative

You pay rent daily. A theta of −0.05 means the option loses ~$5 per contract each day, all else equal — and it speeds up near expiry. The move has to outrun this.

Sellers · theta positive

You collect rent daily. Covered-call and cash-secured-put writers are theta-positive: the passage of time quietly works in their favour.

Delta vs theta.

Most beginner trades are a tug-of-war between these two: delta (your directional gain) pulling one way, theta (decay) dragging the other. Buy an option and you need delta to win the fight before theta wears you down.

05 /
Vega

Vega — sensitivity to volatility.

Vega measures how much your option moves when implied volatility changes by one percentage point. Owning options makes you long vega — you quietly profit if the market grows more fearful, and lose if it calms, even with the stock still.

Long options

Long vega. Rising IV lifts your premium; falling IV (an "IV crush") deflates it. This is the hidden force behind the earnings trap in No. 03.

Longer-dated = more vega

The more time on the option, the more a volatility change is worth. Weeklies barely flinch; multi-month options swing on IV.

Sellers are short vega

Writers of covered calls / cash-secured puts want IV to fall after they sell — another reason selling into high IV is attractive.

06 /
Rho

Rho — sensitivity to interest rates. Usually a footnote.

Rho measures the effect of interest-rate changes. For the short-dated trades a beginner places, it's tiny and safely ignored — it only becomes meaningful for very long-dated options (LEAPS, a year or more out). Know the name; don't lose sleep over it.

Signs

Who feels what.

The whole thing on one grid. For each of the four positions from No. 01, this is the sign of each Greek — the direction each dial pushes you. + helps you as that force rises · − hurts you.

PositionDeltaGammaThetaVegaIn one line
Long call++−+Wants up, fast, into rising vol.
Long put−+−+Wants down, fast, into rising vol.
Short call−−+−Wants flat-to-down, decay, calm.
Short put+−+−Wants flat-to-up, decay, calm.

Notice the symmetry: buyers are +gamma / −theta (they pay for movement), sellers are −gamma / +theta (they're paid to endure it). That single trade-off is most of options in a nutshell.

08 /
In
practice

The Greeks in your starter trades.

Tying it back to the safe five from No. 02 — here's which dial helps and which one bites in each.

Long call / long put

Helps: delta (your move) and vega (rising IV). Bites: theta — every quiet day costs you. Give it time and enough expected move to beat the decay.

Covered call

Helps: theta — you collect decay on the call you sold, and falling IV helps too. Bites: a big rally past your strike caps your gain (that's the deal you signed).

Cash-secured put

Helps: theta and calming IV. Bites: a sharp drop — but you set the cash aside to buy, so you were ready. Sell into higher IV for a fatter premium.

Debit spread

Buying one option and selling another nets down your Greeks — less theta bleed and less vega risk than a lone long option. That muting is exactly why it's a gentler first structured trade.

09 /
Terms

Words you'll meet.

Delta (Δ)

Price change per $1 stock move; also share-equivalent exposure and a rough ITM probability. 0→1 calls, 0→−1 puts.

Gamma (Γ)

How fast delta changes. Highest at-the-money near expiry — the source of last-week whipsaw.

Theta (Θ)

Time decay per day. Negative for buyers, positive for sellers; accelerates toward expiry.

Vega (V)

Price change per 1% change in implied volatility. Long options are long vega; bigger for longer-dated.

Rho (ρ)

Sensitivity to interest rates. Negligible for short-dated trades; matters only for long-dated options.

Long / short vega

Long vega profits when IV rises (option buyers); short vega profits when IV falls (option sellers).

Net Greeks

The combined Greeks of a multi-leg position — spreads deliberately offset legs to shrink risk.

Delta-neutral

A position built so total delta ≈ 0 — no directional bias. An advanced idea; know the term.

LEAPS

Long-dated options (≈1 year+). The place where vega and rho actually start to matter.
