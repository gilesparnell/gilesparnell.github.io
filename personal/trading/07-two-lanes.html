No. 06 — owning & earning

There are two different reasons to sell an option, and they need opposite rules. One is about buying companies you want at a price you chose. The other is about collecting income from companies you have no wish to own. On the screen they look almost identical — which is exactly why people get hurt.

Run them as separate businesses. Different instruments, different cash limits, different definitions of a good day. The rest of this issue is how each one works and how you keep them apart.

The test that sorts every trade

“If I’m assigned these shares, am I pleased?”

Yes → Lane A. Sell a plain put, back it with real cash, and welcome the shares. No → Lane B. Never sell a plain put here — use a spread so the loss has a floor. Answer honestly before you look at the premium, because the premium will always argue for yes.

The real danger.

It is not Lane B going wrong — that is bounded by design. It is Lane A creeping. A company you half-like, at a premium too good to pass up, sold as a plain put because that is what you are used to. Then you are assigned, and you own something you never actually wanted, with the full cash committed. Every trade belongs to one lane. Decide which before you open the chain, not after.

02 / Lane A

Companies you want. The full cycle.

This is the wheel from No. 06, run deliberately rather than mechanically. The whole thing rests on one honest answer: you would be content to own this company at this price.

Step 01

Name your buying price

Not a strike — a price. What would you genuinely be happy to pay for 100 shares? Subtract the premium you’ll collect and that is your real cost. Work backwards from the price you want, never forwards from the premium on offer.

Step 02

Sell the put, park the cash

Collect the premium. Then set the full assignment amount aside in your own accounting. The broker will only hold a fraction — that is your problem to manage, not theirs.

Step 03a — it stays up

Nothing happens

The contract expires. You keep the premium and buy nothing. The cash is released and you can do it again. This is the most common outcome by a wide margin.

Step 03b — it falls

You are assigned

100 shares arrive at the strike. Your real cost is the strike minus the premium. This is not a failure — it is the outcome you agreed to and, in this lane, wanted.

Step 04

Now sell calls against them

You own 100 shares, so you can sell a call and collect again. Keep the strike comfortably above your cost, so that if the shares are taken you are selling at a profit rather than locking in a loss.

Step 05

Called away, or kept

Above the strike, the shares go and you are back to cash with three lots of profit. Below it, you keep the shares and the premium, and sell another call. Either is fine.

03 / When Lane A goes wrong

The shares arrive, and keep falling.

This is the outcome worth rehearsing in advance, because it is the one that tests whether the conviction was real.

You are assigned at $95 on a stock that keeps sliding to $70. You now own 100 shares showing a $2,500 paper loss. Nothing has gone wrong mechanically — the trade did exactly what it said it would. What has happened is that you now hold the company, which is what you said you wanted.

  • Do nothing.If the reason you wanted the company still holds, a lower price is not new information — it is the situation you signed up for.
  • Sell calls above your cost, not above the market.Tempting to write a $75 call for a fat premium. If it is called away you have crystallised the loss. Patience costs premium; it protects the position.
  • Decide whether the story changed.A falling price is not itself a reason to sell. A changed business is. Separate the two before acting.
  • Do not average down automatically.Selling another put to “improve the average” doubles a position that is already going against you. That is the concentration trap in No. 06.

04 / Lane B

Companies you don’t want. Cap the damage.

Here, being handed the shares is the thing you are trying to avoid. So you build the position with a floor under it: sell one put, and at the same time buy a cheaper put lower down. The one you sell earns the income. The one you buy is the safety net.

05 / The numbers

A worked example, in round figures.

Illustrative only — real prices differ every day. A share trading at $100:

Sell the $90 putYou are paid $200The income leg
Buy the $85 putYou pay $80The safety net
Net credit — yours to keep$120The most you can make
Gap between strikes ($5 × 100)$500The size of the risk
Most you can lose ($500 − $120)$380And it stops there
Cash tied up$380Not $9,000
The real advantage.

A plain $90 put ties up $9,000. This spread ties up $380 for a similar chance of success. That is roughly twenty-four positions’ worth of room from the same money — which is what makes genuine spreading across different companies possible at all on a modest account.

06 / What Lane B costs you

Bounded is not the same as safe.

  • You lose the whole risked amount, and not rarely. $380 gone is a common outcome, not a freak one. Capped does not mean unlikely.
  • Less income. You paid for the safety net out of your own premium. $120 instead of $200 on the same move.
  • You may need a higher permission level. Spreads sit above plain puts in most brokers’ tiers. Check yours before planning around them.
  • Two legs, two spreads to cross. Double the commission and double the bid/ask cost, in and out. On thinly traded contracts this quietly eats the credit.
  • You can still be assigned on the leg you sold. The net loss stays capped, but shares can still appear and need dealing with.
  • Nothing to fall back on. In Lane A a bad outcome leaves you holding a company you wanted. Here it leaves you holding nothing.

07 / Running both

Split the money before you split your attention.

The two lanes compete for the same cash, and the one with the better-looking premium will always win that argument in the moment. So decide the split when you are calm, write it down, and treat it as fixed until you deliberately revisit it.

Lane A budget

Few names, large commitments

Each position ties up a full strike’s worth. Count how many simultaneous assignments you could actually absorb — that number, not the premium, is what limits this lane.

Lane B budget

Many names, small commitments

Each position ties up only its own maximum loss. The limit here is attention and correlation, not cash. Twelve spreads on twelve technology companies is one bet, not twelve.

Always keep dry.

Set aside a slice that neither lane may touch. The day everything falls together is the day Lane A hands you shares, Lane B hands you losses, and the best prices in months appear at once. Fully committed, you watch it happen.

09 / The two ways this fails

Both are about lanes bleeding into each other.

Failure one

Lane B priced like Lane A

Selling a plain put on a company you don’t want, because the premium was good and you are in the habit. If it falls you own it, at full cost, with no floor and no reason to hold it. This is the expensive one.

Failure two

Lane A chosen by premium

Letting the chain pick your companies. The richest premium belongs to whatever the market is most frightened of — which is rarely the same list as the companies you actually researched and want to hold for years.

The short version.

Ask one question before every trade: if I’m assigned, am I pleased? Pleased means Lane A — plain put, real cash behind it, few names you know well. Not pleased means Lane B — a spread with a floor, small, spread across names, and never a plain put. Budget the two separately, keep some cash out of both, and write the rules while the market is closed. Nothing here is a recommendation to trade, and none of it is financial advice.
