Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 05

Reading that wall of numbers

The option chain looks like a spreadsheet exploded. It isn’t complicated — it’s a menu, and once you know the shape you’ll never be confused by it again.

What’s in here

  1. It’s a menu with prices down the middle
  2. Two prices, always — and the gap costs you
  3. Is anyone else actually here?
  4. Choosing how long you need
01

It’s a menu with prices down the middle

Every option chain, on every platform, has the same layout. Learn it once.

  • Down the middle runs a ladder of prices — all the possible strike prices you could pick.
  • On one side are the call tickets (the buy ones) for each of those prices.
  • On the other side are the put tickets (the sell ones) for the same prices.
  • Along the top are the dates — each tab is a different expiry.

So reading it is a three-step choice: pick a date, run down to the price you care about, then look left for calls or right for puts. That’s it.

02

Two prices, always — and the gap costs you

Every ticket shows two prices, never one, and this catches everybody out at first.

  • Bid — what someone will pay you if you’re selling.
  • Ask — what you must pay if you’re buying.
  • Spread — the gap between them.
The second-hand shop

The shop will buy your bike for $80 and sell you the identical bike for $100. That $20 gap is how the shop lives.

The bid/ask spread is the same idea. You buy at the higher number and sell at the lower one, so the moment you buy, you’re slightly behind.

A narrow gap is a busy, healthy market. A wide gap means it’ll cost you real money just to get in and back out.

What a wide spread actually means

If the bid is $1.00 and the ask is $1.60, you lose 60 cents per share — $60 a contract — simply for changing your mind. On a ticket worth a couple of hundred dollars, that’s enormous. Wide spreads are a reason to walk away, not a detail.

03

Is anyone else actually here?

Two columns tell you whether a ticket is busy or deserted, and people constantly muddle them.

  • Volume — how many changed hands today. Resets to zero every morning.
  • Open interest — how many exist in total, right now, held by someone.
A restaurant

Volume is how many people came in today. Open interest is how many are sitting at tables right now.

A place with both numbers healthy is somewhere you can get served and leave when you like.

Both numbers near zero is an empty restaurant with the lights off. You might get in. Getting back out at a fair price is the problem.

For anything you might need to exit early — which is most things — check these before you check the premium.

04

Choosing how long you need

Every date tab is a different amount of time, and time cuts differently depending on which side you’re on.

  • Nearby dates are cheap, melt fast, and are twitchy as anything. Great for sellers collecting rent, brutal for buyers.
  • Distant dates cost more, melt slowly, and give your idea room to actually happen. Kinder to buyers, slower going for sellers.
Booking a hotel

One night is cheap but you must be right about tonight specifically. A fortnight costs much more but gives the weather a fair chance to turn.

Buying a very short-dated option is betting on tonight. It’s cheap for a reason, and the reason is that it’s usually wrong.

The words grown-ups use
DTE
Days to expiry.
Weeklies
Options expiring this week — the twitchiest.
Front month
The nearest monthly expiry.
Liquidity
Whether enough people are trading it that you can get in and out sensibly.
