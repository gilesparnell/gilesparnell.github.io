No. 09 — volatility & earnings

Short expiries collect more premium per day. More premium is not the same as more return.

A common piece of advice is to sell 3–6 day options and keep the wheel spinning: put, call, put, call. This page sets out what that buys you, what it costs, what twelve years of index data say about weekly against monthly selling, and how to compare one stock’s premium with another’s.

Why short expiries pay more per day

Time value melts fastest at the end.

An option’s price grows with the square root of time, not with time itself. A one-week option is worth roughly half a one-month option, not a quarter. Sell four weeklies in a row and you collect about twice the premium of one monthly — if every week behaves.

01 / Compare like with like

Compare expiries at the same delta, not the same strike.

A later expiry at the same strike always pays more, partly because it is more likely to be reached. Comparing like with like means holding the risk level constant. Delta is roughly the chance of being called away (No. 09), so pick the strike with the same delta in each expiry.

XYZ at $200, IV 40%. Calls at about 0.30 delta. Illustrative figures.

The same-strike trap

The 14-day $205 looks richer

It pays about $4.00 against $2.40 for the 7-day $205. But its delta is about 0.39, not 0.34. You are being paid more because you are more likely to lose the shares. The fair 14-day comparison is the $207.50.

Where to read it — IBKR

Option chain → Delta column

Switch the expiry tab, then find the call whose delta matches the one you were looking at. The strike will be further out on the longer expiry. Read the premium off that row.

02 / Two weeklies or one fortnightly

Two one-week calls usually beat one two-week call. Usually.

From the table: two 7-day calls at $2.40 bring in about $4.80 if the second week looks like the first. One 14-day call brings in $3.30, fixed today. Only the first week’s premium is known when you start.

Two 7-day callsOne 14-day call
Premium~$4.80 (estimate)$3.30 (known)

When two weeklies come out behind:

  • Called away in week one. There is no second call. You sold at your strike, which is a profit if the strike is above your cost, and you are back to selling puts.
  • The stock falls in week one. Next week’s call has to stay at or above your cost. That strike is now further from the price, so it pays very little.
  • IV drops. Week two’s premium shrinks with it. After earnings, this is the normal path.
03 / What the data say

Weekly selling collected more and returned less.

Cboe publishes two benchmark indexes that sell at-the-money S&P 500 puts, fully backed by cash: one rolls monthly (PUT), the other weekly (WPUT). Research by Prof. Oleg Bondarenko compares them.

Monthly (PUT)Weekly (WPUT)S&P 500
Return a year, 2006–20156.59%5.61%7.09%
Premium collected a year, 2006–201822.1%37.1%—
Volatility, 2006–201810.7%9.5%14.3%
Worst fall from a peak, 2006–201832.7%24.2%50.9%
Read it carefully.

Weekly took in about 1.7× the premium and still returned about 1% a year less: more frequent hits ate the extra income. It was a smoother ride. Risk-adjusted, the two came out about level. This is an index, puts only, before trading costs. Single stocks gap harder, and costs bite four times as often on weeklies. Nothing here tests 3–6 day expiries.

04 / Choosing the stock

Four numbers to compare one stock’s premium with another’s.

The three volatility numbers from No. 09 tell you how much a stock’s options pay and whether now is a good time to sell. A fourth tells you what you earn for the money you tie up.

NumberQuestion it answersHow a seller reads it
IV %How much does the market expect this stock to move?Higher pays more premium — and comes with bigger swings against you.
IV RankIs IV high or low for this stock right now?70+ is expensive for that stock. Low means thin premiums, whatever the IV.
IV / HVAre options priced above what the stock actually does?Well above 100% is a fear premium you get paid to carry. Near 100% is fair.
Premium yieldWhat do I earn per dollar tied up?Premium ÷ (strike × 100), at the same delta and expiry for every stock.
Worked read — premium yield

The bigger cheque isn’t the better deal

ABC at $400: a 30-day 0.30-delta call pays $8 — $800 on $40,000 of shares, 2.0%. DEF at $100: the same call pays $2.50 — $250 on $10,000, 2.5%. DEF pays less per contract and more per dollar.

Where to find them — IBKR

One watchlist, the same columns

Put every stock you are considering in one watchlist with Implied Vol. %, 52 Week IV Rank and Implied Vol./Hist. Vol %. The Screeners section can rank stocks by option implied volatility. Premium yield you work out from the chain.

05 / One bet, not four

Different tickers can still be the same trade.

Large technology and growth stocks tend to fall together. Selling puts on four of them is closer to one bet four times the size than four separate bets. A broad sell-off hits all of them in the same week.

  • Add up every obligation. Strike × 100 × contracts, across every open put on every stock, against your cash. The leverage check in the glossary applies to the total, not each trade.
  • Calmer names pay less for a reason. A lower-IV stock pays less at the same delta because it is less likely to gap. That is a feature, not a flaw.
  • Each stock has its own earnings week. IV rises before every report and drops straight after. More stocks means more of those weeks to plan around (No. 09).

07 / The ticket

Before you press Submit.

  • The delta, not just the strikeIf you changed the expiry, re-pick the strike from the Delta column.
  • Limit at the midEvery $0.01 is $1 a contract. On a $3.75 / $3.90 market, selling at the bid instead of the ask is $15 less. Start at the mid and step toward the bid.
  • The market is openUS options trade only in the regular session — 6½ hours. The glossary has the times in Sydney, which shift through the year.
  • Wait out the openPrices jump around for the first 15–30 minutes. A limit placed after that is more likely to fill near fair value.
The short version.

Short expiries collect more per day, give you less room and more chances to be called away, and cost more in trading. Compare expiries at the same delta, compare stocks by premium yield, and add up what you owe across every stock. Nothing here is a recommendation to trade, and none of it is financial advice; it is a map of how the mechanics fit together if you choose to use them.

Sources: O. Bondarenko, An Analysis of Index Option Writing with Monthly and Weekly Rollover (2016) and Historical Performance of Put-Writing Strategies (2019), both for Cboe.
