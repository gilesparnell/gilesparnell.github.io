Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 02

How not to get hurt

There is one habit underneath this whole page: work out the worst thing that can happen before you start, in actual dollars, and make sure it’s a number you could shrug off.

What’s in here

  1. Some risks have a bottom. Some don’t.
  2. Make the loss small enough to be boring
  3. If you sold a promise, someone can call it in
  4. The questions to ask before every single trade
  5. Things that mean stop
01

Some risks have a bottom. Some don’t.

Before any strategy, learn to tell these two apart, because it’s the difference between a bad afternoon and a genuine disaster.

  • Risk with a bottom. You know the very worst outcome before you start. You paid $200 for a ticket; if everything goes wrong you lose $200 and not a cent more. You can sleep.
  • Risk without a bottom. There’s no floor under it. You sold a promise, and if things go badly wrong the bill keeps growing. You cannot know the worst case, because there isn’t one.
A fairground way to think about it

The first kind is a ride with a seatbelt. It might be unpleasant, but there’s a limit to what happens to you.

The second kind is standing up on the roller-coaster. Usually fine! Right up until it very much isn’t.

When you’re learning, stay in the first kind. Every single time.

The words grown-ups use
Defined risk
Risk with a bottom. You know your worst case.
Undefined / naked risk
Risk without a bottom. Avoid while learning.
Max loss
The worst case, in dollars, written down before you start.
02

Make the loss small enough to be boring

You will get trades wrong. Not might — will. Everyone does. So the skill isn’t avoiding losses, it’s making each one small enough that being wrong doesn’t matter much.

The sweets jar

Imagine all your money is a big jar of sweets. One trade should risk a small handful — the kind of amount where losing it is annoying rather than upsetting.

If losing a trade would genuinely ruin your month, the trade is too big. Not too risky — too big. Same trade, smaller size, is often completely fine.

A common rule people use is that no single trade risks more than one or two percent of the total. The exact number matters less than having one at all, decided in advance, when you’re calm.

03

If you sold a promise, someone can call it in

When you sell a ticket, you’ve made a promise. The other person can decide to hold you to it, and here’s the uncomfortable part: they choose when, not you.

Most people assume this only happens on the expiry date. It doesn’t. In the US market it can happen on any trading day. It becomes much more likely when the ticket is deeply worth using.

The thing to actually do

Never sell a promise you couldn’t keep today, without warning. Promised to buy 100 shares at $139? The $13,900 needs to be genuinely available now, not next week. If it isn’t, don’t make the promise.

The words grown-ups use
Assignment
Being made to keep your promise.
Early assignment
Being made to keep it before the expiry date — which is allowed.
Expiry / expiration
The date the ticket dies.
Exercise
The other person choosing to use their ticket.
04

The questions to ask before every single trade

Boring, and it’s the boring bit that keeps people solvent. Go through these every time, ideally out loud.

  • What is the very worst that can happen, in dollars? If you can’t answer instantly, stop. You don’t understand the trade yet.
  • Could I shrug that off? Not “is it unlikely” — could you actually absorb it if it happened tomorrow?
  • Am I looking at real, live prices? Delayed prices mean you’re guessing.
  • Is anyone else actually trading this? If almost nobody is, you may not be able to get back out.
  • Why am I doing this? “It looked like easy money” is not a reason. It’s a warning.
05

Things that mean stop

If you catch yourself doing any of these, the answer is to close the laptop, not to push on.

  • Trying to win it back. Making a bigger trade to undo a loss. This is the single most expensive habit in trading.
  • Not being able to explain it. If you can’t say in plain words what happens if the price goes up, down, or nowhere, you’re not ready to place it.
  • Being talked into it. Someone online being very confident is not research.
  • Using money you need. Rent money and options must never meet.
  • Selling promises you couldn’t keep. Covered in the section above, and worth repeating because it’s how people blow up.
The honest test

Before you press the button, say the trade out loud as a sentence, as though explaining it to a friend who knows nothing.

“I’m promising to buy 100 of these at $139 each — that’s $13,900 — and I’m being paid $210 for the promise.”

If saying it out loud makes you wince, that’s useful information. Listen to the wince.
