The basics

What an option actually is, and the words on the contract. 17 terms

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
OptionA contract giving its buyer the right — but not the obligation — to buy or sell an underlying asset at a fixed price before a fixed date.A ticket that says you’re allowed to do something later, at a price agreed today. You don’t own the thing; you own the choice.You pay $5 for a ticket letting you buy a bike at $100 any time this month. If the bike jumps to $150, great. If it drops, you’ve lost $5.
UnderlyingThe asset an option is written on — usually 100 shares of a specific stock.The actual thing your ticket is about.For a TSLA option, the underlying is Tesla shares.
CallAn option giving the right to buy the underlying at the strike price.A ticket that lets you buy at a set price. You want one if you think the price is going up.A $150 call lets you buy at $150 even if the stock has risen to $180.
PutAn option giving the right to sell the underlying at the strike price.A ticket that lets you sell at a set price. Useful if you think the price is falling, or as insurance on shares you own.A $140 put lets you sell at $140 even if the stock has fallen to $110.
Strike priceThe fixed price written into the contract at which the underlying may be bought or sold.The price printed on the ticket. It doesn’t change, whatever the market does.In “SPCX Sep04 $139 put”, the strike is $139.
PremiumThe price of the option itself — paid by the buyer, received by the seller.What the ticket costs. The buyer hands it over; the seller keeps it whatever happens next.A premium of $2.10 costs $210, because one contract covers 100 shares.
ExpiryThe date the contract ceases to exist. After it, the option is worthless or has been exercised.The date the ticket stops working. Like a train ticket — after that, it’s just paper.“Sep04’26” means the contract dies on 4 September 2026.
ContractThe unit of trading. One equity option contract almost always represents 100 shares.Options come in packs of a hundred. You can’t buy just one share’s worth.A quote of $2.10 means $210 per contract. A $139 strike means a $13,900 commitment.
Holder (long)The party who bought the option and holds the right to exercise it.The person holding the ticket. They get to choose whether to use it.Buy a call and you’re the holder — you decide, and your worst case is losing the premium.
Writer (short)The party who sold the option and carries the obligation to perform if exercised.The person who sold the ticket. They took the money, so they don’t get a vote later.Sell a put and you must buy the shares at the strike if the holder says so.
ExerciseThe holder choosing to use their right, converting the option into the underlying transaction.Actually using your ticket rather than letting it expire.Exercising a $150 call means buying 100 shares at $150, costing $15,000.
AssignmentBeing selected to fulfil an obligation because the option you sold has been exercised.Getting the phone call telling you to keep your promise. You can’t decline.You sold a $139 put; the stock falls to $120; you are assigned and must buy 100 shares at $139.
American styleAn option that may be exercised on any business day up to and including expiry. US equity options are American style.The other person can use their ticket whenever they like, not only on the last day.Your short put expiring in three weeks could be assigned tomorrow if the stock drops sharply.
MoneynessWhere the current price sits relative to the strike — whether exercising now would be worthwhile.Is the ticket worth using right this second?A $100 call is “in the money” when the stock trades at $130.
In the money (ITM)An option with intrinsic value: a call whose strike is below the current price, or a put whose strike is above it.Using the ticket right now would actually help you.Stock at $130, your $100 call is $30 in the money.
Out of the money (OTM)An option with no intrinsic value — exercising now would be worse than trading at market.Using the ticket now would be silly. It can still be worth something, because there’s time left.Stock at $70, your $100 call is out of the money — you’d simply buy at $70 instead.
At the money (ATM)An option whose strike sits at or very near the current price of the underlying.The ticket price and the real price are basically the same, so using it changes nothing.Stock at $100.20 and a $100 strike — near enough at the money.

Reading the screen

The chain, the columns, and how orders are placed. 12 terms

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
Option chainThe table listing every available strike and expiry for an underlying, with live prices for each.The menu. Calls on one side, puts on the other, prices down the middle, dates across the top.Pick a date tab, run down to the strike you want, look left for calls or right for puts.
BidThe highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay. What you receive if you sell now.What someone will give you for your ticket.Bid $1.77 means selling right now gets you $177 per contract.
AskThe lowest price a seller will currently accept. What you pay if you buy now.What you must hand over to get the ticket.Ask $1.82 means buying right now costs you $182 per contract.
SpreadThe gap between bid and ask. An immediate cost of entering and exiting.The shop buys your bike for $80 and sells you the same bike for $100. That $20 gap is the spread.Bid $1.00 / ask $1.60 means a 60¢ spread — $60 a contract lost simply changing your mind.
VolumeThe number of contracts traded today. Resets to zero each morning.How many people came into the shop today.Volume of 3 means almost nobody is trading this contract.
Open interestThe total number of contracts currently open and held by someone.How many people are sitting at tables right now, rather than how many walked in today.Open interest of 4,060 means plenty of positions exist — usually easier to trade.
LiquidityHow easily a contract can be bought or sold near its quoted price, judged from spread, volume and open interest.Whether there are enough people around that you can get in and out without being stung.Tight spread plus healthy volume equals good liquidity. Both near zero means you may be stuck.
Limit orderAn instruction to trade only at your specified price or better. May not fill.“I’ll do it, but only at this price.” You might wait forever, but you never get a nasty surprise.Sell limit at $2.22 when the bid is $2.20 — the order rests until someone pays $2.22.
Market orderAn instruction to trade immediately at whatever price is available.“Just do it now, whatever it costs.” Fast, and occasionally expensive.On a wide spread, a market order can fill far worse than the price you were looking at.
Time in forceHow long an order stays active — commonly DAY (expires at the close) or GTC (good till cancelled).How long your instruction stands before it’s thrown away.DAY means the order dies at the end of today’s session if it hasn’t filled.
DTEDays to expiry — how much time the contract has left.How much ice is left in the ice cube.A contract expiring Friday has 4 DTE on the Monday.
WeekliesContracts expiring at the end of a given week rather than monthly. Short-dated and twitchy.The very short tickets. Cheap, and usually wrong.The “W” beside a date on the chain marks a weekly expiry.

Price & the Greeks

What the premium is made of, and what moves it. 11 terms

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
Intrinsic valueThe amount by which an option is in the money. Pure arithmetic, no opinion involved.What the ticket would be worth if you used it this second.Stock at $130, $100 call: intrinsic value is $30.
Extrinsic valueEverything in the premium beyond intrinsic value — what people pay for the chance of further gain. Also called time value.The hope part. It’s what people pay for “it might get even better.”A $38 premium with $30 intrinsic has $8 of extrinsic value.
Time decayThe steady erosion of extrinsic value as expiry approaches, accelerating near the end.An ice cube melting. Slowly at first, then all at once near the end.A contract can lose value over a quiet week even though the share price barely moved.
VolatilityHow much and how fast the underlying moves. Higher volatility means more expensive options.How bouncy the thing is. Bouncy things have dearer tickets, because anything might happen.A steady utility has cheap options; a stock that swings 10% a day has expensive ones.
Implied volatility (IV)The market’s expectation of future movement, backed out of current option prices.The weather forecast baked into the price. Not what happened — what people are betting will happen.IV of 55% signals the market expects considerable movement from here.
IV crushA sharp fall in implied volatility once an anticipated event passes, cutting option prices.Everyone was braced for drama, the drama happened, and now nobody needs the insurance.You call an earnings direction correctly and still lose, because every option got cheaper at once.
DeltaThe rate of change of the option price relative to a $1 move in the underlying. Roughly approximates the chance of finishing in the money.How tight the tow rope is. Delta 0.9 means you get dragged nearly as far as the stock moves; 0.1 means you barely twitch.Delta −0.28 on a put means it gains about 28¢ if the stock falls $1.
GammaThe rate at which delta itself changes as the underlying moves.How violently the gears change when the road tilts. High gamma means your position feels different minute to minute.Gamma is highest near the strike and near expiry — which is why the last day feels wild.
ThetaThe value lost per day from time decay, all else equal.Daily rent. Buyers pay it; sellers collect it.Theta of −0.05 means the option loses about $5 per contract per day if nothing else changes.
VegaSensitivity of the option price to a change in implied volatility.How much you care about the forecast changing. You own umbrellas; storms make them worth more.High vega means a calm market quietly costs you money even if the price doesn’t move.
RhoSensitivity to interest rate changes. Usually the least significant Greek for short-dated equity options.The dial that barely moves. Safe to ignore while learning.A rate change of a quarter point makes almost no difference to a four-day option.

Positions & strategies

The named trades, and what each one commits you to. 9 terms

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
Covered callSelling a call against 100 shares you already own, in the same account.“You can buy my card for $10 this month. Pay me 50¢ now.” You must actually own the card.Own 100 shares at $360, sell a $450 call, keep the premium unless it rises past $450.
Cash-secured putSelling a put while holding enough cash to buy the shares if assigned.“I’ll buy your card for $5 if you want to sell. Pay me 50¢ now.” You need the $5 ready.Sell a $139 put and keep $13,900 available in case you’re assigned.
Naked optionA short option not backed by the shares or cash needed to meet the obligation.Promising to sell a card you haven’t got. The bill has no ceiling.Selling a call on shares held at a different broker is naked, not covered.
Protective putBuying a put on shares you own, to set a floor under their value.Insurance. It costs money, and you hope you never need it.Own shares at $360, buy a $320 put, and you can always sell at $320 however far it falls.
CollarHolding shares while buying a protective put and selling a call to help pay for it.Buy insurance, and sell off some of your upside to cover the bill.Own at $360, buy the $320 put, sell the $420 call — floored below, capped above.
The wheelRepeatedly selling cash-secured puts until assigned, then selling covered calls until the shares are called away.A loop: cash → promise to buy → shares → promise to sell → cash. Round and round.Assigned at $139, then sell $160 calls against the shares; if called away, start again with cash.
Debit spreadBuying one option and selling another further out, for a net cost. Both gain and loss are capped.You buy a ticket and sell a cheaper one to help pay for it. Smaller win possible, smaller loss too.Buy the $100 call, sell the $110 call — the most you can lose is what you paid.
Credit spreadSelling one option and buying another further out for protection, receiving a net credit.You sell a promise, then buy a smaller promise as a safety net. Caps the damage.Sell the $95 put, buy the $90 put — the $90 put limits how bad it can get.
RollingClosing an existing option and opening a similar one at a different strike or expiry.Swapping your ticket for a later or different one. Fine as an adjustment, dangerous as a rescue.Buy back a put expiring Friday and sell one expiring next month instead.

Risk & safety

Knowing the worst case before you start. 7 terms

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
Defined riskA position whose maximum loss is fixed and known before entry.A ride with a seatbelt. It might be unpleasant, but there’s a limit.Buying a call for $200 — the very worst outcome is losing that $200.
Undefined riskA position whose loss has no fixed ceiling.Standing up on the roller-coaster. Usually fine, until it very much isn’t.A naked call: the stock can keep rising, and your loss rises with it.
Max lossThe worst realistic outcome, in dollars, calculated before entering.The number you write down first. If you can’t work it out, you don’t understand the trade.Short $139 put: the theoretical worst case is $13,900 minus the premium received.
Position sizingChoosing trade size so that any single loss is survivable.Only ever risking a small handful from the sweets jar.Many people cap one trade at 1–2% of the total account.
Concentration riskHolding several positions that all suffer from the same event.Two bets that look separate but are really one bet, twice the size.Owning a lot of a stock and also selling puts on it — a fall hurts both at once.
Early assignmentBeing assigned before expiry, which American-style options permit at any time.The promise gets called in sooner than you expected. You don’t choose when.A deeply in-the-money short put can be assigned days before its expiry date.
Paper tradingPractising with simulated money on a real platform.Playing the game with pretend money until the buttons stop being scary.Most brokers offer a paper account — worth using for longer than feels necessary.

Broker & platform

Margin, market data, and the settings that bite. 10 terms

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
Options approval levelThe tier a broker grants you, controlling which strategies you may trade.Permission levels. You earn the risky ones slowly.Lower tiers allow covered calls; naked options require the highest tier.
Initial marginThe collateral a broker requires when a position is opened.The deposit the broker takes up front to hold the position.A short put with a $13,900 obligation might require around $4,190 of initial margin.
Maintenance marginThe ongoing collateral required to keep a position open. Falling below triggers a margin call.The amount you must keep in the account, or they start closing things for you.Maintenance of $3,762 sits just below the initial requirement on the same position.
Cash-secured vs margin-securedWhether the full assignment value is genuinely set aside, or the broker is only holding a fraction of it.“Cash-secured” is usually a rule you impose on yourself, not one the platform enforces.A broker reserving $4,190 against a $13,900 obligation will happily let you sell more than your cash covers.
Buying powerThe total value of positions you could open, including borrowed capacity.How much the platform will let you commit — which is not the same as how much you should.Buying power well above your cash balance means leverage is available. It magnifies losses too.
Market data subscriptionA paid feed giving real-time prices. Not included by default at every broker.You have to pay for live prices. Otherwise you’re looking at old ones.New accounts often show delayed data until a subscription is added.
OPRAThe consolidated feed carrying US listed options quotes. Required for real-time option prices.The pipe that live options prices come down. No free version exists.Without OPRA, an option chain shows prices up to fifteen minutes old.
Delayed dataQuotes shown on a lag, typically fifteen minutes, when no real-time subscription is held.Old prices. Fine for browsing, dangerous for placing orders.Setting a limit against a delayed quote means pricing against a market that has already moved.
Non-professional statusA market-data classification for individuals trading their own money, at far lower data fees than the professional tier.Are you doing this for yourself, or for a business? The honest answer sets the price.Using a data feed for a commercial product makes you professional, whatever the account size.
SMART routingA broker feature that automatically selects which exchange to send an order to.The platform picks which shop to take your order to.Most retail option orders route this way by default.

Your portfolio screen

Every field on the IBKR summary bar, Positions and Orders tabs — with the real numbers from a live account. 20 terms

Read these two first

Of everything on that summary bar, two numbers carry the weight. Excess Liquidity answers “am I safe?” — it is the distance between you and forced liquidation. Net Liquidation answers “am I making progress?” — it is the whole account marked to market. Daily P&L is mostly noise, and Unrealized P&L is only as trustworthy as the price feed behind it. The figures below are taken from a real account holding a single short put, so they hang together as one consistent picture.

TermDefinitionLike I’m 10Example
Net LiquidationWhat the account would be worth if every position were closed right now at current prices.The honest answer to “what have I actually got?”$64,615.92 — cash plus the current value of the open position.
Excess LiquidityHow far the account sits above the point at which the broker begins closing positions without asking.Your safety cushion. The number to check before any other.$60.9K — a wide buffer. If this heads toward zero, positions get liquidated for you.
Initial MarginCollateral required at the moment a position is opened.The deposit taken up front to let you hold the position.$4.2K held against a $13,900 obligation — roughly 30%, not the full amount.
Maintenance MarginThe ongoing collateral that must remain in the account to keep a position open.The amount you have to keep there, or they start closing things.$3.7K — slightly below the $4.2K initial requirement on the same position.
Available FundsNet liquidation minus the initial margin already committed. What is free for new positions.What’s left to play with.$60.5K — the $64.6K account less the $4.2K tied up.
Buying PowerThe total value of new positions that could be opened, including any borrowed capacity.What the platform will let you commit — which is not the same as what you should.$60.5K, equal to Available Funds. When it is a multiple of that, leverage is switched on.
Unrealized P&LProfit or loss on open positions at current marks, not yet banked.Money you’re winning on paper. It can still go away.+$62.26 — the short put sold for $210 is now worth about $142.
Realized P&LProfit or loss locked in by closing a position.Money actually banked. This only changes when you close something.$0.00 — nothing has been closed yet, so nothing is realised.
Daily P&LChange in value since today’s open. Resets each morning.Just today’s wiggle. Usually noise.−$4.45 (−0.01%) on a day when the position moved slightly against you.
Position (negative)The number of contracts held. A negative number means the position is short — sold, not owned.A minus sign means it’s a promise you made, not a thing you have.−1 means one contract sold short: one obligation outstanding.
Cost Basis (negative)What the position cost to establish. Negative when a credit was received rather than paid.Minus means money came to you.−$209 — $210 collected, less about 80¢ commission.
Avg PriceThe average price at which the position was opened, net of commission.What you really got per share after costs.2.092 rather than the 2.10 filled at — the difference is commission.
Last (C prefix)The most recent price. A leading “C” marks it as a closing price rather than a live trade.That letter means “this is yesterday’s number, not what’s happening now”.C1.42 — the mark used for P&L while the market is shut.
FROZEN / DELAYEDFrozen means quotes are not streaming, usually because the market is closed. Delayed means they lag by around fifteen minutes.Two different ways of saying: these prices are not live.FROZEN DELAYED means every P&L figure on screen is calculated off a stale mark.
Bid / Ask sizeThe number of contracts available at each quoted price, shown beside it.How many are actually for sale at that price. A small number means a thin market.22 x 1.41 bid, 89 x 1.44 ask — only 22 contracts wanted at the bid.
Buy to closeBuying back a contract you previously sold, to cancel the obligation and flatten the position.Undoing your promise by buying it back off the market.Short −1 at $209 received; buying back near the $1.44 ask costs about $144.
Sell to openSelling a contract you do not hold, creating a new short position.Making a fresh promise. Doing this twice makes two promises, not zero.Pressing Sell again on a −1 position takes you to −2, doubling the obligation to $27,800.
Order statusWhether an instruction filled, is still working, or was cancelled at the end of its session.Did it actually happen, or not?Three orders showed Cancelled with 0/1 filled; one showed Filled at 2.10.
Quantity 0/1Filled quantity over requested quantity on an order.How many you got, out of how many you asked for. 0/1 means it never happened.0/1 on the TSLA $350 puts — requested one, filled none, then cancelled.
Fill priceThe price an order actually executed at, which may differ from the limit requested.What you really got, rather than what you asked for.Limit 2.10, fill 2.10 — matched exactly. A 0.00 fill means nothing traded.

Nothing matches that. Try a shorter word.

A reminder. This is a learning reference written in plain language on purpose. It is education, not financial advice. Options can lose you more than you put in, and nothing here is a suggestion to make any particular trade.
