Everything in this series — strike, premium, moneyness, the Greeks, volatility — shows up in one place on a trading platform: the option chain. At first it's a wall of numbers. It's really just a menu, and once you know the columns you can read any broker's version. Here's the whole thing, decoded.

The
layout

Calls on one side, puts on the other, strikes down the middle.

02 /
The
columns

What every column is telling you.

Brokers vary the order and let you add or hide columns, but these are the ones that matter. Read them in two groups: price (what you'll pay or receive) and health (whether the option is safe to trade at all).

Price columns
BidHighest price a buyer will pay — what you'd receive if selling now.
AskLowest price a seller will accept — what you'd pay to buy now.
MidMidpoint of bid & ask — a fair-value estimate, often where you aim.
LastPrice of the most recent actual trade. Can be stale.
ChangeMove since the prior close. Context, not decision.
Health & risk columns
VolumeToday's contracts traded. Activity right now.
Open int. (OI)Contracts currently open. Depth of the market.
IVImplied volatility — how pricey the option is (No. 03).
DeltaExposure + rough ITM odds (No. 04). Great for picking a strike.
Θ / VTheta & vega, if shown — your decay and volatility risk.
First glance.

Before anything else, a beginner should look at two columns: the bid–ask spread (is it tight?) and open interest (is it healthy?). Those tell you whether the option is even worth trading before you ever think about the price. The next page is all about them.

03 /
The
spread

Bid, ask & the spread — the hidden toll.

You buy at the ask and sell at the bid. The gap between them is the spread — a cost you pay just to get in and out, before the trade even moves. Wide spreads quietly tax every round trip; tight spreads are the mark of a liquid, tradeable option.

A tight spread vs a wide one — same $3.30 option
Use limit orders

Never buy "at market" on options. Set a limit near the mid and let the market come to you — market orders on wide spreads get filled at ugly prices.

Mind slippage

The difference between the mid you hoped for and the price you actually got. Wide spreads = more slippage on both entry and exit.

A quick filter

If the spread is more than a few percent of the option's price, or the ask is far above the bid, walk away — there's usually a tighter strike or a more liquid stock.

04 /
Vol &
OI

Volume vs open interest.

Two liquidity gauges that people mix up. Together they tell you how easy it'll be to get in and — more importantly — back out.

Volume

Contracts traded today. A fresh pulse of activity — it resets to zero each morning. High volume means people are trading this strike right now.

Open interest

Contracts currently held open. The standing pool of positions. High OI means a deep, established market you can exit into later.

Rule of thumb: favour strikes with healthy OI (think hundreds-to-thousands, not single digits) and real daily volume. Thin options can be easy to enter and a nightmare to escape.

05 /
Money-
ness

Moneyness, at a glance.

The shading pattern on the chain isn't decoration — it's telling you moneyness. On the call side, strikes below the stock are in the money; on the put side, strikes above it are. They cross at the at-the-money row. Delta is the cleaner readout: the closer to the money, the closer delta is to 0.50.

In the money · high delta

Has intrinsic value, moves closely with the stock, costs more. Delta well above 0.50. Behaves the most stock-like.

At the money · ~0.50 delta

All time value, maximum gamma and theta. The most "leveraged to a move" — and the most exposed to decay.

Out of the money · low delta

No intrinsic value, cheap, low odds. Tempting because it's cheap — but it's mostly a bet against decay and time.

06 /
Expiry

Choosing the expiration.

The date you pick first sets how much time — and time value — is in play. Most chains offer weekly and monthly expirations; the countdown is shown as DTE (days to expiry).

Very short (0–7 DTE)

Cheap and twitchy — maximum decay and gamma. Popular with gamblers, unforgiving for learners. The series' advice: avoid as a beginner.

30–45+ DTE

Enough time to be right without decay racing you, and typically the most liquid monthly strikes. A sensible default window for starter trades.

Match the expiry to your thesis: if you think a move plays out over a month, don't buy a week of time and force yourself to be right in days.

07 /
Worked
read

A worked read — building a safe debit spread.

Let's turn the page-1 chain into an actual defined-risk trade from No. 02. Say you're mildly bullish on this $100 stock over the next month. Here's the read, step by step.

1 · ExpiryPick the ~30-day date — enough time, and the most liquid strikes.
2 · Buy legBuy the $100 call at the ask, 3.45. Delta ~0.52 — a balanced directional bet.
3 · Sell legSell the $105 call at the bid, 1.35. This caps upside but cuts your cost.
4 · Net cost3.45 − 1.35 = 2.10 debit → $210 per spread. That's your max loss, fixed.
5 · Max gainStrike width $5 − $2.10 = $2.90 → $290, if the stock closes at/above $105.
6 · Health checkBoth strikes show tight spreads and OI in the thousands — liquid enough to enter and exit.

Before clicking, run it past the No. 02 checklist: max loss known ($210), affordable and ≤1–2% of the account, defined-risk, liquid, with an exit plan. Numbers are illustrative — but the reading process is exactly this every time.

08 /
Terms

Words you'll meet.

Option chain

The full grid of calls and puts across strikes and expirations for one underlying.

Bid / ask

Sell price / buy price at this moment. You transact against these, not "last".

Spread

Ask minus bid — the built-in cost of a round trip. Tight = liquid.

Mid

The midpoint of bid and ask; a fair-value target for limit orders.

Volume

Contracts traded today at that strike. Resets each session.

Open interest

Contracts currently open — the depth and staying-power of the market.

Slippage

The gap between your intended price and the fill you actually get.

Limit order

An order capped at a price you set. The default, and only, way to trade options carefully.

DTE

Days to expiration — the countdown shown against each expiry date.
