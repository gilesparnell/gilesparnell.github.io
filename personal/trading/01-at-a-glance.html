A contract on something else, with two sides you can take. Most people only ever picture themselves as the buyer — but for every option that trades, someone is also a seller. Both seats are real strategies. Both are how money moves.

Anatomy

An option is a contract on an underlying asset.

Two people sit on either side of a piece of paper. One pays a premium upfront for the right to do something later. The other pockets the premium and accepts the obligation to play along if asked.

Either
side

An option has two sides. You can take either one.

This is the part most people forget. When you trade an option, you are choosing a seat — buyer or seller. Both are real positions, both have premium flowing through them, and both are how traders actually make money. The buyer side is just the half that gets advertised.

Door 1 · the familiar side

You buy the option

Become the holder — long the option

You pay the premiumcash out today
You hold a rightto buy (call) or sell (put)
You decideexercise, sell, or let expire
Max losspremium paid

You're betting that something happens — a move, a spike, a specific outcome — before the deadline.

Door 2 · the forgotten side

You sell the option

Become the writer — short the option

You collect the premiumcash in today
You take an obligationto deliver / take delivery
The buyer decidesyou wait
Max gainpremium received

You're betting that nothing happens — the move doesn't come, time passes, the option expires unused.

The 50/50.

For every option that exists, someone bought it and someone sold it. Premium flowed from one to the other. If "buying options" is the only side you consider, you're looking at half of every trade — and ignoring the side that, by structure, takes in cash on day one.

03 /
Two
flavors

Calls go up. Puts go down.

There are only two kinds of options. A call is a contract about buying the underlying; a put is a contract about selling it. Either flavor can itself be bought or sold — which produces the four positions on the next page.

Call · C

A right to buy.

The holder can buy the underlying at the strike, regardless of where the market goes. Think of it as a coupon: "good for one share at $100, until next month." Worth more the higher the share trades.

ViewBullish — wants price up
Profits whenspot > strike + premium
Put · P

A right to sell.

The holder can sell the underlying at the strike, even if the market price has collapsed. Think of it as insurance: "good for one share sold at $100, even if it's trading at $60." Worth more the lower it falls.

ViewBearish — wants price down
Profits whenspot < strike − premium

Every option is a mirror.

The seller's payoff is exactly the negative of the buyer's. Whatever the holder makes at expiration, the writer loses — and the other way around. Choosing which side to take is choosing which half of this picture you want.

05 /
Who
does
what

Rights belong to the buyer. Obligations belong to the seller.

This is the single asymmetry that defines every option. The buyer chooses; the seller waits. The seller is compensated for that asymmetry by the premium — that's what they're being paid for.

Rights · the buyer (holder)

Right to exercise — invoke the contract at the strike. Right to walk away — if exercising would lose, let it expire; max loss is the premium. Right to close — sell the option to someone else anytime. No obligation, ever — exercise is optional. That's the whole point of being long.

Obligations · the seller (writer)

Must perform if assigned — deliver (call) or take delivery (put) at the strike, even if the market's far away. Keeps the premium no matter what. Can buy back to close — repurchase the option to extinguish the obligation. No choice in the matter — if exercised against, must comply.

Glossary

A few more words you'll meet.

Premium

The price the buyer pays the seller for the contract. The seller keeps it regardless.

Strike (K)

The agreed-on price for the underlying if the option is exercised.

Expiration

The date after which the option ceases to exist. Weekly, monthly, quarterly cadences.

Underlying

The asset the option is written on — stock, ETF, index, future, or commodity.

Exercise

Using the right: buying (call) or selling (put) at the strike. A holder's choice.

Assignment

The seller's side of exercise — being forced to deliver. Not optional.

Intrinsic value

The payoff you'd get if exercised right now. Zero for out-of-the-money options.

Extrinsic / time value

Premium beyond intrinsic — paid for the possibility the option moves further in before expiry.

American vs European

American: exercisable any time before expiry. European: only at expiry. Most single-stock options are American.

Long / short

Long = the buyer's side, holding the right. Short = the seller's side, carrying the obligation.

Covered vs naked

A short call is "covered" if the seller owns the underlying — limited risk. "Naked" if they don't — risk unbounded.

Contract multiplier

Standard US equity options represent 100 shares per contract. A $1 premium quote = $100 paid.
