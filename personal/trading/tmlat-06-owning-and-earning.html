Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 06

Getting paid for promises

This one is about using options around things you actually want to keep. The rule underneath all of it: you can only promise what you’ve genuinely got.

01

Money lets you promise to buy. Stuff lets you promise to sell.

The one rule

Only sell puts on things you’d want to own.

Selling a put is a promise to buy the shares. If the price drops, you own them — that is the deal, not bad luck. This one rule turns a loss into a purchase you were happy to make.

This is the whole page in one idea, and everything else follows from it.

Football cards

Your friend has a card you’d love. You say: “If you ever want to sell it, I’ll buy it off you for $5. Give me 50 cents now and that’s a deal.” To make that promise honestly, you need the $5. Money backs a promise to buy.

Now flip it. You own a card already. You say: “You can buy this off me for $10 any time this month. Give me 50 cents now.” To make that promise honestly, you need the card. Stuff backs a promise to sell.

So what you hold decides what you can do. Only cash? You can promise to buy. Only shares? You can promise to sell. Both? Both.

The words grown-ups use
Sell a put
Promising to buy. Backed by cash.
Covered call
Promising to sell something you own. Backed by shares.
Naked call
Promising to sell something you don’t own. Dangerous. Avoid.
Collateral
The cash or shares that make your promise real.
02

Getting paid, and what you gave up for it

Both promises pay you straight away. Both have a catch, and the catches are mirror images.

  • Promise to buy (sell a put). Best case, nothing happens and you keep the money for nothing. Worst case, the thing crashes and you must buy it anyway at the price you promised — now well above what it’s worth.
  • Promise to sell (covered call). Best case, nothing happens and you keep the money and your shares. Worst case, it soars and you must hand your shares over at your agreed price, missing the whole rise.
The uncomfortable truth

In both cases the money you collect is small and certain, and the thing that can go wrong is large and unlikely.

That’s not a flaw someone forgot to fix. It’s the deal. You are being paid a little, reliably, for taking on something painful, rarely. If that trade-off ever looks free, you’ve misread it.

03

The one rule people break: the stuff has to be in the right cupboard

You cannot back a promise with something held somewhere else.

Two cupboards

You keep your football cards at your gran’s house. At school you promise a friend they can buy one for $10.

You do own a card. But it isn’t here, and the promise is here. As far as anyone at school is concerned, you’ve promised something you haven’t got.

In real life

If your shares sit with one broker and you sell a call at another, that call is not covered — it’s naked, which is the dangerous kind with no ceiling on the loss. The shares must live in the same account as the option. To use them, move them there first.

04

The wheel — and why you might not want to spin it fast

Chain the two promises together and you get a loop that people call the wheel:

  • Start with cash. Promise to buy at a price you’d be happy with, and get paid for the promise.
  • Either nothing happens and you keep the money — or you get the shares at your price.
  • Now you own shares, so promise to sell them a bit higher, and get paid again.
  • Either nothing happens and you keep both — or they’re bought off you and you’re back to cash.
  • Repeat.
The catch nobody mentions

The wheel assumes you don’t much mind whether you own the thing — it treats shares like stock in a shop.

But if you genuinely want to own a company for years, spinning fast works against you: you keep selling your shares away exactly when they’re doing well.

The fix is to promise to sell only at prices far above today’s. You collect less. You also keep the thing you wanted in the first place.

05

The trap: promising more of what you already have too much of

Say you already own a lot of one company. Promising to buy even more of it, cheaper, feels clever — it’s a company you know and like.

But think about when that promise gets called in. It gets called in when the price falls. Which is precisely when the pile you already own is worth less.

All your eggs, and then some

You’re carrying a basket of eggs. You promise that if anyone drops eggs, you’ll buy those too.

The day someone drops eggs is the day eggs are going badly for everyone — including the ones already in your basket.

So you end up with more of the thing that’s hurting you, automatically, without deciding to.

Two positions that looked separate turn out to be the same bet, just bigger. Spreading promises across different companies isn’t fussiness — it’s the entire point.

06

“As much income as possible” is two wishes fighting

It’s tempting to say: make me as much monthly income as you can, without risking my money. Those are two different wishes and they pull against each other.

  • Promise at prices closer to today’s and you’re paid more — and get held to it far more often.
  • Make more promises and you’re paid more — and could be held to all of them at once, on the same bad day.
  • Pick wilder companies and you’re paid more — because they genuinely might crash.
A better wish

Swap it for: “pay me steadily, without ever threatening the thing I actually want to own.”

That version can answer “should I do this trade?” with a no. The maximum-income version always answers yes — which is how people end up forced to sell the very thing they were trying to keep.

07

When you actually want the shares

Everything above treats getting handed the shares as the thing to avoid. But sometimes it’s the whole point — there are companies you’d be pleased to own. When that’s true, the money you were paid means something completely different.

Think of it like this

You promise to buy your neighbour’s bike for $350 next month, and they hand you $45 for making the promise.

The bike arrives. Did you get $45 of pocket money? No — you bought a bike for $305. The $45 was never separate. It was money off the price.

So if you want the shares, the payment isn’t income at all. It’s a discount. And that changes what a good trade looks like.

The words grown-ups use
Assignment
The shares actually being handed to you. Here, the goal — not the accident.
Cost basis
What the shares really cost you, once the payment is taken off.
Delta
Roughly the chance you end up with the shares. The dial that decides everything below.

Here’s the surprising bit. When you don’t want the shares, “get paid more” and “don’t get stuck with it” fight each other. When you do want them, they stop fighting. Promising a price closer to today’s pays you more and makes the shares far more likely to turn up. Both wishes finally point the same way.

08

Making sure you really end up with them

Saying you want the shares isn’t enough. The promises you make have to match.

  • Check the dial. If every promise you’ve made has about a 1-in-10 chance of landing, you’ll almost never get shares. That’s fine — it’s just a money-collecting trade, not a buying one. Around 1-in-3 is where buying starts to happen.
  • You can promise above today’s price. Shares are $150 and you promise to pay $155, and they hand you $8 for it. You almost certainly get the shares — and they cost you $147, less than buying them outright today.
  • Don’t wriggle out at the last second. When it looks like the shares are coming, the screen shows red and everything in you wants to push the promise further away. Don’t. Take the shares. That was the plan.
  • Go easy on selling promises against them afterwards. Once you own the shares you can get paid again by promising to sell them. But then someone takes them away — and you’re back to cash in the exact company you spent months collecting.
  • Count what you could actually pay for. Assume every promise lands in the same week, because on a bad day they all do. Could you afford all of them? If not, make fewer.
The bit nobody shows you

If you really want something, the sure way to own it is to just buy it. Promising gets it a bit cheaper if it comes to you — and leaves you sitting in cash if it doesn’t. If the shares climb from $375 to $600 and never dip, you collected small payments all year and never got on board. No screen anywhere shows you the shares you didn’t buy.
