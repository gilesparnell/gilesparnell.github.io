Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 03

Why the ticket price keeps changing

A ticket’s price is made of two things glued together: what it’s worth right now, and hope. The first is simple maths. The second is where all the confusion lives.

What’s in here

  1. Every price is two things added together
  2. The hope part melts, and it speeds up
  3. Bouncy things have dearer tickets
  4. How to be right and still lose money
01

Every price is two things added together

Whenever you see an option price, you’re looking at a sum. Two parts, always:

  • Real worth right now. If you used the ticket this second, how much better off would you be? Your call lets you buy at $100, the thing costs $130, so that’s $30 of real worth. No opinion involved — it’s arithmetic.
  • Hope. Everything else. This is what people will pay for the chance that things get even better before the ticket expires.

If a ticket costs $38 and only $30 of that is real worth, the other $8 is pure hope. And hope has a nasty property, which is the next section.

The words grown-ups use
Intrinsic value
The real worth right now. The arithmetic part.
Extrinsic value
The hope part. Also called time value.
Premium
The two added together. What you actually pay.
02

The hope part melts, and it speeds up

Hope value shrinks every single day, because there’s less time left for something good to happen. On expiry day it reaches exactly zero — there’s no future left to hope about.

The ice cube

Think of the hope part as an ice cube sitting on a table. It melts whether or not anything interesting happens.

And it doesn’t melt evenly. A big cube on a cold morning barely changes. A small cube in the afternoon sun vanishes fast.

Options behave the same way: a ticket with three months left loses hope value slowly. A ticket with three days left loses it very quickly.

This cuts both ways, which is what makes it interesting. If you bought the ticket, melting is your enemy — you’re losing money just from time passing. If you sold the ticket, melting is your friend, and it’s the whole reason people sell options for income.

The words grown-ups use
Time decay
The melting.
Theta
The Greek letter for how fast it melts per day. See No. 04.
DTE
Days to expiry — how much ice is left.
03

Bouncy things have dearer tickets

Two companies. Both cost $100 a share. One drifts up and down by a few cents a week. The other regularly leaps or dives 10% in a day.

A ticket on the bouncy one costs much more. Not because anyone knows which way it’s going — nobody does — but because anything might happen, and tickets are worth more when anything might happen.

The weather

You’re selling umbrella insurance. Charging someone in a place where it never rains is easy money, so you charge very little.

In a place with wild, unpredictable storms, you charge a lot — because you might genuinely have to pay out.

Option prices work identically. Wild and unpredictable means expensive tickets.

The trap hiding in this

When you spot an option paying a wonderfully fat premium, the market is not being generous. It is telling you, in the only language it has, that this thing moves violently. The rich premium is the warning label.

The words grown-ups use
Volatility
How much and how fast something jumps about.
Implied volatility (IV)
How bouncy the market is betting it’ll be from here.
IV crush
When a big event passes, uncertainty vanishes, and ticket prices drop suddenly — even if you guessed the direction right.
04

How to be right and still lose money

This is the one that genuinely baffles beginners, and it’s worth sitting with until it clicks.

The maddening example

You buy a call because you reckon the shares will rise. You’re right — they rise! And your ticket is worth less than when you bought it.

How? Two things worked against you while you were being right. The hope value melted a bit each day. And the market got calmer, so everyone’s tickets got cheaper.

Both losses were bigger than your small win from the price going up. You were correct about the direction and it didn’t save you.

With shares this can’t happen: right about direction means money. With options, you need to be right about direction, size of move, timing, and to not get caught by calm settling in. Four things instead of one.

That’s not a reason to avoid options. It is the reason that “I think this will go up” is a badly incomplete reason to buy a call.
