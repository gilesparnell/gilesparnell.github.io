Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 08

Getting out, on purpose

Every page before this one is about starting a trade. This one is about ending it. A sold option has three endings, and only one of them is something you choose.

What’s in here

  1. The three ways it can end
  2. Why leaving early isn’t chickening out
  3. How much have you already made?
  4. Doing nothing is still a choice
  5. How you actually close one
  6. Rolling — the third door
01

The three ways it can end

You sold someone a promise. That promise has an end date printed on it. When the date arrives, exactly one of three things happens.

  • You buy the promise back early. You pay whatever it is worth today, and the promise is torn up. This is the only ending where you pick the day.
  • The date passes and nobody wants it. The price stayed on the good side, so the promise is worthless and just disappears. You keep all the money. You do nothing at all.
  • The date passes and they use it. The price ended on the bad side, so the shares get pushed onto you and the money leaves your account. Automatically. Nobody checks with you first.
The bit people get wrong

There is no fourth ending where you ignore it and nothing happens. “I’ll just leave it” means picking ending two or ending three blind, based on where the price lands in the last few minutes.

02

Why leaving early isn’t chickening out

It feels wrong to close a trade that is winning. There is still money left on it. Why give that up?

Think of it like this

Someone pays you $50 to carry their very expensive vase across town. By the time you reach the last street corner you have earned $43 of it. There is $7 to go.

You are still carrying the vase. It is exactly as heavy, and exactly as breakable, as it was at the start. Putting it down now isn’t giving up $7. It is refusing to carry a vase for $7.

That is the whole idea. When you sold the option, you were promising to buy $35,000 of shares in exchange for $456. Once the promise is only worth $105, you are still promising the same $35,000 — but there is only $105 left to earn.

The reward got smaller. The risk did not.

The words grown-ups use
Buy to close
Buying the promise back to end it. You sold to open, so you buy to close.
Capturing profit
How much of the total possible money you have already banked.
Realised
Money that is actually yours now, not just showing on a screen.
03

How much have you already made?

This is the number to look at, and it is easy to work out.

  • What you were paid when you sold it. Say $456. That is the most you can ever make on this trade — there is no way to earn more than that.
  • What it costs to buy back right now. Say $105.
  • The difference is your profit so far. $456 − $105 = $351, which is 77% of everything the trade will ever pay.

Rough guide, and it really is only a guide:

  • Under half made? Leave it. It is still earning properly.
  • Between half and three quarters? This is the normal window to think about buying it back.
  • Over three quarters? The money left is small and the promise is still full size. Usually time to go.
Except when it’s worth pennies

If the promise is only worth $7, buying it back costs $7 plus about a dollar in fees. That is $8 spent to protect $7. At that point, just let it run out — as long as the price is nowhere near the line.

04

Doing nothing is still a choice

Say you leave it alone and let the date arrive. Here is what actually happens.

  • Price ended on the good side. The promise expires. It vanishes from your account overnight. No button to press, no fee, and the money that was being held aside is freed up.
  • Price ended on the bad side — even by one cent. The shares are pushed onto you automatically. 100 of them, and the cash goes out. One cent over the line counts the same as five dollars over the line.
Think of it like this

You promised your neighbour you would buy their bike for $100 any time before Friday, and they paid you $5 for the promise.

If bikes are worth $120 on Friday, they keep their bike and you keep the $5. Nobody knocks on your door.

If bikes are worth $99 on Friday, the bike is on your doorstep on Saturday morning and $100 has gone. They don’t ask if it still suits you.

Watch out for

The last hour of the last day is when prices move most and there is the least time to do anything about it. And in Australia that hour lands around 6am — so if you plan to act on the final day, decide in advance whether you are actually going to be awake for it.

05

How you actually close one

Selling started it, so buying ends it. Same company, same date, same strike price, same number of contracts.

  • Find the position in your Positions list. Start from the row itself, not from searching again — then there is nothing to type wrong.
  • Right-click it and pick Close Position. Your broker fills in the order for you, already set to buy.
  • Check it says BUY. Selling again by mistake gives you two promises instead of none. This is the expensive slip.
  • Say the most you’ll pay. Never just say “any price”. Options have a wide gap between the buy and sell price, and “any price” means you accept whatever is sitting there.
  • Start in the middle and creep up. If people are buying at $1.00 and selling at $1.15, offer $1.07 and wait. Nothing? Try $1.10.
  • Check it worked. The row should be gone, and your banked profit should have moved. Until both of those happen, you still have the promise.
Remember the second-hand shop?

From No. 05. The shop buys from you cheap and sells to you dear, and the difference is how it makes a living.

You met that gap when you sold the option, and you meet it again when you buy it back. You pay it twice. Nobody ever labels it as a fee, which is why it is easy to forget it is there.

06

Rolling — the third door

There is one more move. You buy back the promise that is about to run out, and sell a new one with a later date, both in a single go. That is called rolling.

Think of it like this

It is asking for an extension on your homework. Sometimes that is genuinely the right call — more time is exactly what you need, and here you usually get paid a bit extra for taking it.

But the homework is still not done. You have moved the problem, not solved it. Ask for enough extensions and one day it is a very big piece of homework.

The honest test

If you are rolling to dodge getting shares in a company you originally said you’d be happy to own — you have quietly changed your mind about the company, and the roll is hiding it from you.

The one to remember

The risk doesn’t shrink as the profit does.

Once most of the money is made, you are carrying the same heavy vase for a lot less. That is the moment to put it down.
