Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 10

How long, and on what

Every time you sell an option you choose two things: how long the promise lasts, and which company it’s on. Short promises pay you more per day. That doesn’t always mean you end up with more.

01

Renting out your bike by the day

Think of it like this

You rent out your bike. Renting it for one day costs more per day than renting it for a whole month. So if you rent it out a day at a time, every day, you collect more money in total.

Options work the same way. A one-week option is worth about half a one-month option, not a quarter. Sell four one-week options in a row and you can collect about twice as much as one monthly one.

The catch

Every rental is another chance for something to go wrong, and more paperwork each time. More money coming in isn’t the same as more money kept.

02

Compare the same chance, not the same line

A longer option at the same price line always pays more. Part of the reason: the stock has more time to reach your line, so you’re more likely to have to hand over your shares.

To compare fairly, pick the line with the same chance of being reached in each option. The number for that chance is delta. If you’re comparing 0.30 in one week, compare it with 0.30 in the other.

  • Stock at $200. One-week call at $205 pays $2.40, about a 1 in 3 chance.
  • Two-week call at the same $205 pays $4.00 — but the chance is about 4 in 10.
  • The fair two-week comparison is the $207.50 line, which pays $3.30.
03

Two short rentals or one long one?

Two one-week calls at $2.40 bring in about $4.80, if next week looks like this week. One two-week call brings in $3.30, and you know that today.

  • Two weeks of one-week calls: more money, but about a 1 in 2 chance your shares get taken at some point.
  • One two-week call: less money, about a 1 in 3 chance, and only one decision.

If the stock drops in the first week, your second call pays very little, because you still want your line above what you paid for the shares.

04

What happened when people tried both

A big exchange (Cboe) tracks two pretend accounts that sell the same kind of option on the whole US stock market. One sells weekly, one sells monthly.

  • The weekly one collected about 1.7 times as much money.
  • But over ten years it grew about 1% a year less than the monthly one.
  • It was a smoother ride, with smaller drops.

All those extra rentals meant more crashes, and the crashes ate the extra money.

05

Which bike earns the most?

Bouncy stocks pay more for options, because they’re more likely to jump. Calm ones pay less. Three numbers from No. 09 tell you how bouncy and whether right now is a good time. One more tells you what you earn for your money:

  • Premium yield = what the option pays ÷ what the shares cost.
  • A $400 stock whose call pays $8: $800 on $40,000. That’s 2%.
  • A $100 stock whose call pays $2.50: $250 on $10,000. That’s 2.5% — smaller cheque, better deal.
The words grown-ups use
Delta
Roughly the chance your line gets reached.
Premium yield
What the option pays for every dollar the shares cost.
Premium per day
What the option pays, divided by how many days it lasts.
06

Four bikes, one road

Big technology companies tend to go up and down together. Selling options on four of them is like renting out four bikes that all ride the same road. If the road floods, all four get wet at once.

Watch out for

Add up everything you’ve promised to buy across every company, and check you have the cash for all of it at the same time. Not one at a time — all of it.

The one to remember

More money coming in isn’t more money kept.

Compare options with the same chance, compare companies by what they pay per dollar, and add up everything you’ve promised.
