Tell me like a 10-year-old · No. 04

The Greeks are just dials

They have intimidating names because they’re Greek letters. They are just dials on a dashboard, each one answering a single plain question about your ticket.

What’s in here

  1. Five dials, five questions
  2. Delta — how much you move with it
  3. Gamma — how twitchy delta is
  4. Theta — the daily rent
  5. Vega — how much you care about drama
  6. Which way each dial points for you
01

Five dials, five questions

Nobody sat down to make this confusing. Each Greek letter answers one question, and the questions are all fairly obvious once someone tells you what they are.

  • Delta — if the share moves $1, how much does my ticket move?
  • Gamma — how quickly does that answer itself change?
  • Theta — how much do I lose each day just from time passing?
  • Vega — how much do I care if things get more or less jumpy?
  • Rho — how much do I care about interest rates? (Almost never. Ignore it for now.)

Learn delta and theta properly and you’ll understand most of what happens to your positions. The others are refinements.

02

Delta — how much you move with it

Delta is a number between 0 and 1 (or 0 and −1 for puts). It says how much your ticket moves when the share moves by $1.

Being towed on a rope

Delta 0.5 means you’re on a fairly slack rope. The share walks forward a metre, you get dragged half a metre.

Delta 0.9 means the rope is nearly tight — the share moves a metre, you move ninety centimetres. You’re almost being the share.

Delta 0.1 means a very long, loose rope. The share moves a whole metre and you barely twitch.

There’s a handy second use: delta is roughly the chance the ticket ends up worth using. A delta of 0.3 means somewhere near a 30% chance. It’s an approximation, not a promise — but it’s how people talk about picking strikes, so it’s worth knowing.

03

Gamma — how twitchy delta is

Delta doesn’t sit still. As the share price moves, delta changes too. Gamma measures how fast.

Bicycle gears

Delta is what gear you’re in. Gamma is how violently the gears change when the road tilts.

Low gamma is a smooth, predictable ride. High gamma means the gears crash about — one small move in the share and your position feels completely different from a moment ago.

Gamma is highest for tickets that are close to the strike price and close to expiry. That combination is exactly why last-day options feel wild and unmanageable: tiny movements flip you between “worthless” and “valuable” repeatedly.

04

Theta — the daily rent

Theta is the ice cube from No. 03, put into a number. It tells you how much value drips away per day, all else being equal.

Renting a deckchair

You pay for the deckchair by the day whether or not you sit in it. Theta is that daily charge.

If you bought the ticket, you’re the one paying rent. Every morning you wake up slightly poorer, even if nothing happened.

If you sold the ticket, you’re the landlord collecting it. Every quiet day is a good day.

This is the real reason people sell options for income: they’re collecting rent from time itself. And it’s the real reason buying far-out cheap options usually disappoints — you’re paying rent on something that mostly expires worthless.

05

Vega — how much you care about drama

Vega says how much your ticket’s price changes when the market’s expectation of jumpiness changes.

The umbrella shop

You own a shop full of umbrellas. A forecast of storms arrives — you haven’t sold a thing, but your stock just became more valuable. Then the forecast changes to a calm fortnight and your umbrellas are worth less again.

Nothing about the umbrellas changed. Only expectations did. That’s vega.

It explains the classic beginner injury around big announcements: everyone expects drama, tickets get expensive, the announcement happens, uncertainty evaporates, and every ticket gets cheaper at once — including the ones held by people who guessed the direction correctly.

06

Which way each dial points for you

The same dial helps one person and hurts another, depending on whether you bought or sold. This little table is worth remembering:

  • You bought a ticket: time hurts you (theta against), calm hurts you (vega against), big moves help you.
  • You sold a ticket: time helps you (theta for), calm helps you (vega for), big moves hurt you.
The plain-English version

Buyers need something to happen, and reasonably soon. Boredom is expensive for them.

Sellers need nothing much to happen. They are being paid for patience, and drama is what costs them.
